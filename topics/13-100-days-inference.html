Start here (no maths needed)

Learn one thing every day for 100 days, write down what you learned, and share it. Small steps add up to expertise.

Think of it like this

It's like a "100 days of drawing" challenge. You draw something every day and post it. Day 1 looks rough, and by day 100 you're far better. You can also look back and see exactly how you got there. Here, the "drawing" is understanding how AI models are served to millions of people.

How it works

  1. Pick a guide (here, a book about inference engineering).
  2. Each day, read or build one small piece.
  3. Write a few lines about what you learned, in your own words.
  4. Share it publicly, so others can learn and correct you.
  5. Tick the day off. Keep the streak going, but don't panic if you miss one.

Why robots care

Robots run AI models on a small computer with strict time limits. "Inference engineering" is the skill of making models answer fast and cheaply, and that's exactly what a robot needs.

1. Summary

TL;DR

A new GitHub journal, "100 days of Inference Engineering", works through Philip Kiely's book chapter by chapter. Kiely is the Baseten author already quoted in Module 05.

The first two entries cover the inference stack (runtime plus infrastructure), shared vs dedicated deployment, model choice, application-specific evals, fine-tuning vs distillation, and the core latency metrics.

The book's eight chapters line up almost one-to-one with Modules 09 and 12, so the journal works as a reading track alongside the course.

From Baseten's page: a book for engineers who want to understand the technologies behind AI applications. Kiely says it distils four years of work at Baseten, his docs and talks, and interviews with Baseten engineers. 3. The journal so far

Summarised in my own words from the README (Days 1–2 at the time of fetching):

  • Day 1 (chapter 0):
    • Inference engineering is young; it was a niche specialty only a few years ago.
    • Since around 2024, open models have been catching up with closed ones. The DeepSeek V3/R1 moment is the example. As a result, many companies fine-tune open models for domain tasks.
    • A production stack needs runtime optimisations, infrastructure optimisations and developer tooling.
  • Day 2 (chapter 1):
    • There's no one-size-fits-all setup. Know your model requirements, interface, latency budget, unit economics and usage pattern.
    • Choose between shared (pay-per-token) inference and dedicated deployments. If the workload is unknown, start shared.
    • Model choice is the biggest lever: try a smaller model before reaching for tricks like speculative decoding.
    • Distrust gamed benchmarks; build application-specific evals.
    • Fine-tuning changes weights only, while distillation moves behaviour into a different, smaller architecture.
    • The metrics: TTFT, perceived and total tokens/s, inter-token latency, and percentiles.

Each idea already has a deeper page here: model choice and cost (09 · Problem 24), application evals (01, 08 Stage 10), distillation (04, 12 · video 05), and metrics and percentiles (09 · Problem 17).

The journal is two days old, so expect it to grow. Re-ingest it later to fold new days in.

5. How to learn in public well

  • Write in your own words. Paraphrasing is where understanding happens (Module 03, desirable difficulty). It also avoids copying the book.
  • Add one number a day: a measurement, a calculation, a benchmark. Numbers make notes useful later.
  • Say what you didn't understand. Readers will help, and it shows honest progress.
  • Publish runs, not just reading notes: configs, logs and what didn't work (Module 08 Stage 12).
  • Respect sources: credit the book, and don't repost its figures without permission.

6. Why it matters for robotics

  • Latency budgets are hard deadlines on robots. TTFT and inter-token latency become reaction time.
  • "Try a smaller model first" is doubly true on a robot's limited compute. Distillation is the main route (Module 04).
  • Edge vs cloud is the robot's "shared vs dedicated" choice. Run fast reflexes on board, and send slow, heavy reasoning to a server.
  • Fleet-scale serving chapters (autoscaling, multi-tenancy) matter mostly for cloud-side robot services, not the robot itself.

7. Self-check

When should you start with shared (pay-per-token) inference instead of a dedicated deployment?

When you don't yet know your workload's volume and shape. Shared inference avoids paying for idle GPUs while you learn.

What's the difference between fine-tuning and distillation, as the journal notes from the book?

Fine-tuning retargets a model by changing its weights only. Distillation transfers a large model's behaviour into a smaller, different architecture.

Why look at latency percentiles, not just averages?

Averages hide the slow tail. Users and SLOs feel the p95/p99. See Module 09, Problem 17.

What's the cheapest optimisation to try before speculative decoding?

A smaller model that still passes your application evals.
