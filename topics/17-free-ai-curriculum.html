2. Provenance: where the list comes from

The article's content matches Deep Learning Drizzle, a GitHub list by kmario23 (created Nov 2018, about 13,000 stars, still updated in 2026). The evidence is in the article itself:

  • Its "Go to Contents ⤴️" links point to github.com/kmario23/deep-learning-drizzle#contents.
  • It ends with the repo's own "To-Do" list, including unfinished sections on autonomous vehicles and computer graphics.

The article doesn't credit the repo. Credit belongs to Deep Learning Drizzle's maintainer and to the hundreds of instructors who made their courses free. The repo has no licence file, so this module links to it for the canonical, maintained version. The catalog below is a personal, searchable index of the course names and links, with link-health checks added.

3. Reality check

  • "Replaces your degree": free lectures can teach the material, but a degree also brings structure, assessment, feedback, peers and a credential. Courses with assignments (CS231n, CS224n, CS285, CS336) come closest. Watching videos alone doesn't.
  • "5000+ lectures": that's plausible given 391 courses, but quantity isn't the point. Nobody needs 5,000 lectures. A focused path of 5–10 courses with exercises beats it.
  • Freshness: the list stops at 2022. The foundations (backprop, CNNs, RL theory, probabilistic models) haven't changed. LLM practice has changed completely since 2022, and so has robot learning (VLAs, diffusion policies).
  • Link rot is real but modest: about 1 in 8 links are dead, and 10 courses have no working link left.

7. Self-check

Why credit Deep Learning Drizzle rather than the article?

The article's own internal links and its To-Do list come from that repo. The list was compiled and maintained there since 2018.

What fraction of courses still have at least one working link?

381 of 391 (about 97%), even though about 1 in 8 individual links are dead.

Which major topics are missing because the list stops in 2022?

Modern LLM training at scale, post-training and alignment, LLM serving and inference, agents, and robot foundation models (VLAs, diffusion policies). There's also no robotics section at all.

What makes a free course closest to "replacing a degree"?

Graded assignments and projects you actually do, in a sequence, ideally with peers or feedback. Watching lectures isn't enough.
