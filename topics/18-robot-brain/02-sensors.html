1. The idea

💡 The idea: look along a line

Your robot only finds walls by bumping into them. It needs to look ahead. That's your week 1 loop again: look at the next square; if it's free, count it and look further.

Blocks don't stop the beam in this simple world. Only walls do.

💡 The idea: noise, and why averaging works

Ask friends to guess how long a corridor is. Some guess too high, some too low. Average the guesses and the mistakes partly cancel. Real distance sensors (ultrasonic, infrared, laser) are the same: they never say exactly 7. Here are 8 real noisy readings of a true distance of 7:

The worst single reading is off by 0.73. The average is off by 0.10. Bigger groups do better, but slowly. Here's the typical error from the homework file, over 500 tries each:

Noise wobbles both ways, bias leans one way. Right next to a wall the true distance is 0, and a reading can't go below 0. So the wobble can only push it up, and the average comes out a little too big. Here are 16 real readings with noise 0.5 (seed 1):

Averaging cancels noise, but not bias. You'll see this bias in your robot's look.

Why only half? The ups and downs only partly cancel. And averaging costs time: a moving robot can't wait for 64 readings. Engineers pick a balance. Your robot uses 16.

5. Real-world challenge: be the noisy sensor

🌍 Real-world challenge
  1. Pick a hallway or garden. Pace it out 16 times, writing down each count. Don't measure it yet.
  2. Now measure it with a tape measure, and measure one of your steps. Turn each count into metres.
  3. Find the error of each single pace-out, |reading − true|, and average them: that's N = 1.
  4. Split the 16 into four groups of 4. Average each group, find its error, and average those: that's N = 4. Then all 16 together.

On your own: this works alone. No hallway? Measure your desk 16 times with a short ruler you have to move along.

The error should shrink as N grows. If it doesn't, look for a bias: maybe all your paces were short. Averaging cancels wobble, not a sensor that's always wrong the same way.

Check yourself

What's the difference between noise and bias?

Noise wobbles up and down; averaging cancels it. Bias pushes every reading the same way; you fix it by calibrating against a known true value.

Predict: distance_to_wall(ROOM, (5, 3), "W")?

1. (4, 3) is free, then (3, 3) is wall.

Predict: the typical error for 256 readings?

About 0.03. Four times as many as 64 halves 0.056.

Why might a fast robot not want 64 readings?

By the time it has them, it has moved, and the wall is closer than its "accurate" answer.

Want to know more?

Seeds: random numbers you can repeat 
import random
rng = random.Random(1); print(rng.uniform(-1, 1))   # -0.7312715117751976
rng = random.Random(1); print(rng.uniform(-1, 1))   # -0.7312715117751976 again

That's how your output can match this page. The 8 readings in the picture are noisy_reading(7, 1.0, rng) with seed 1.

The square root rule

The error shrinks like 1 ÷ √n. √4 = 2, √16 = 4, √64 = 8: so 4, 16 and 64 readings give about a half, a quarter and an eighth of the single-reading error.
