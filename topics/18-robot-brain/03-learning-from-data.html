Learning from data: fit a line by shrinking the error

Don't give the robot a rule. Give it measurements, and let it nudge a rule until it fits. That loop is how every modern AI learns.

Start here

Learning means: guess a rule, measure how wrong it is, nudge it to be a bit less wrong, repeat.

Model
A rule with adjustable numbers. Ours: distance = w × speed + b.
Mean squared error (MSE)
One number for "how wrong". 0 is perfect.
Learning rate
How big a nudge you take each time.

1. The idea

💡 The idea: draw the best line

Your robot slides after it brakes, and the faster it goes, the further it slides. It has 13 real measurements: "at this speed, I slid this far". They lie almost on a straight line. A line has two numbers: w (how steep) and b (where it starts). Learning means finding good values for them.

To score a line, take each miss, square it (so too-high and too-low both count, and big misses count a lot), then average. The shaded squares show it: a miss twice as long makes a square four times as big. MSE = the average area of these squares, the mean squared error. The guess above scores 0.0293. The learned line scores 0.00036.

💡 The idea: walk downhill in the fog

You're on a foggy hillside and want the bottom of the valley. You can't see it, but you can feel the slope under your feet. So: step downhill, feel again, step again. Here the height is the error, and the slope is called the gradient.

● Green: small steps walk down to the bottom. ● Red: steps too big jump over the valley and climb higher.

Each step: work out the gradient, then move w and b a little bit the other way. The step size is the learning rate. That loop is gradient descent. Swap "two numbers" for "billions" and "a line" for "a neural network", and it's how ChatGPT and Claude were trained.

🤖 Add it to your robot

From squares to metres. One square is 0.5 m. Use the smallest true distance (the closest wall is the dangerous one), and count the robot's own square too: free metres = squares × 0.5 + 0.5. At the start the closest wall is north, 0 squares away, so 0.5 m. Does the robot stop in time?

5. Real-world challenge: the ramp

🌍 Real-world challenge
  1. Lean a board or big book on a stack of books. The number of books (1 to 5) is your "speed".
  2. Let a ball or toy car roll down (don't push) and measure how far it goes past the bottom. Do 2–3 rolls per height and average them (Part A!).
  3. Plot it on squared paper, draw your best line with a ruler, and read off w and b.
  4. Type your data into Python and run fit_line(my_data, lr=0.01). If history grows instead of shrinking, divide lr by 10.
  5. Predict how far it rolls from 6 books. Then test it.
On your own: this works alone. No ball? A marble, a round pen or a coin on its edge. No tape measure? Measure in floor tiles.

The best line isn't the one through every point. It's the one with the smallest squared misses. Predicting something you never measured is the whole point.

Check yourself

Why square the misses instead of just averaging them?

Plus and minus misses would cancel, so a bad line could score 0. Squaring makes every miss count, and big ones count most.

dw = +3.0 and db = −0.5. Which way does each move?

Against the gradient: w goes down, b goes up. With lr = 0.1: w changes by −0.3, b by +0.05.

Predict: mse(2, -1, [(1, 1), (2, 3)])?

0. The line hits both points exactly.

Predict: max_safe_speed(0.810, 0.071, 1.5)?

1.5. You need 0.810 × s + 0.271 ≤ 1.5, so s ≤ 1.517.

Want to know more?

Gradient descent by hand, one step

On (1, 1) and (2, 3) at w = 0, b = 0, the gradients are −7 and −4. With lr = 0.1:

w = 0 - 0.1 × (-7) = 0.7
b = 0 - 0.1 × (-4) = 0.4
new MSE: misses 0.1 and -1.2 → (0.01 + 1.44) / 2 = 0.725   (down from 5)

Repeat 2,000 times and you reach w = 2, b = −1, through both points.

Feeling the slope without a formula

Nudge w a tiny bit and see how the error changes. On the robot data: MSE at w = 0 is 1.0938, at w = 0.01 it's 1.0686. Change per unit of w ≈ −2.52. The formula gives −2.53. The 2 in the formula comes from the squaring; you'll meet why in calculus.

What "too big a step" really does

With lr = 0.5 on the robot data, the error goes 1.95, 3.51, 6.33… and after 30 steps it's in the millions. Each step jumps over the valley to a higher point. That's called diverging.

  • Write fit_line_until(data, lr, tol) that stops when one step improves the error by less than tol. Compare lr = 0.01, 0.1, 0.3.
Common mistakes
  • Walking uphill: w + lr * dw. The checker says the learned w should be about 0.81 or the error should go DOWN during training.
  • Updating w before working out db. Get both gradients first.
  • Learning rate too big: huge numbers or OverflowError. Make it 10 times smaller.
