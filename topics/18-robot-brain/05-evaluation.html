Is it any good? Honest testing, precision and recall

Test on examples the robot never saw, count each kind of mistake, and make the dangerous one rare.

Week 4 of 16 · Part B. So far your robot: ✓ says hello ✓ moves ✓ senses walls ✓ knows its braking distance ✓ reads real phone data ✓ decides to stop. Today it learns to prove its stop decisions are safe. Next: Week 5: Finding the way. Your robot, week by week.

Start here

A smoke alarm that beeps at burnt toast is annoying. One that stays quiet in a fire is a disaster. So alarms are jumpy on purpose. We'll make the robot's brakes jumpy too.

Test set
Examples hidden from the model until the end, for a fair exam.
False alarm
Said "stop", but there was no need. Annoying.
Miss
Said "go", but should have stopped. Dangerous.
Precision
Of the times it said stop, how many were real?
Recall
Of the real stops, how many did it catch?
Threshold
How sure it must be before it says stop. Part A used 0.5.

1. The idea

💡 The idea: hide some examples

Part A's neuron got 96.2% right, but on the same examples it learned from. That's like seeing the exam answers first. So before training, shuffle the examples and lock 25% in a drawer (the test set). Train on the rest. Score on the drawer.

And don't trust accuracy alone. In our 150 test examples, 37 need a stop. A lazy robot that never stops is right on the other 113: 75.3% accurate, while hitting every single obstacle.

💡 The idea: count two kinds of right

Here are the 150 test examples at two thresholds (real numbers from lesson05_evaluate.py). The dashed box is everything the robot said STOP to.

● needed a stop · ○ fine to go (inside the box: a false alarm) · red ring: missed. The 111 or 106 correct "go"s are left out.

Lower the threshold and the box gets bigger: it catches more real stops (recall up) but also more false alarms (precision down). A false alarm costs a pause. A miss hits the cat. So the rule is: recall at least 0.97, and the highest threshold that manages it.

Pick that threshold using the training examples only. If you tune anything on the test set, it isn't a fair exam any more.

5. Real-world challenge: will it cross?

🌍 Real-world challenge

Measure a human's precision and recall. Clear a safe stretch of floor and put tape across it about 2 m away. Use a soft ball or rolled-up socks.

  1. Round 1 (20 rolls). As the ball leaves your hand, a friend calls "crosses!" (1) or "stops!" (0). Write down the call and the truth.
  2. Fill in TP, FP, FN, TN. Work out accuracy, precision and recall.
  3. Round 2. New rule: "a missed 'crosses' means the ball hits a baby, so only say 'stops!' if you're sure." You've lowered their threshold. Did recall go up and precision go down?

On your own: before each roll, write your own call from how hard you're about to push. Then roll and score both rounds the same way.

Check yourself

A model has 0 false alarms. Is it safe? Its precision is 1.0, but it could miss lots of stops. At threshold 0.9 precision is 1.000 and 8 stops are missed. Safety is recall.

confusion([0, 0, 0], [1, 1, 0])? (0, 0, 2, 1): two misses, one correct go. Precision and recall are both 0.0.

Why choose the threshold on TRAIN? The test set stands in for brand-new situations. Tune on it and its score stops being honest.

Want to know more?

The full trade on TEST:

threshold   precision   recall   accuracy
   0.9        1.000      0.784     0.947
   0.7        1.000      0.946     0.987
   0.5        0.946      0.946     0.973
   0.3        0.878      0.973     0.960
   0.22       0.837      0.973     0.947
   0.1        0.804      1.000     0.940

0.7 has the best test accuracy, but picking it by looking at the test set would be cheating. On TRAIN, 0.5 misses 11 of 110 stops (recall 0.900); 0.22 catches 107 of 110 (0.973).

The one miss left at 0.22 is the reading (2.79, 1.38), stop chance 0.186: sensor noise made danger look calm. No threshold above 0.186 can catch it, which is why real robots also have bumpers.

Why does the robot say 0.17, not 0.22? It trains on different examples, made with random.Random(0), so it learns slightly different weights and its safest threshold comes out different too.

A surprise: at 0.5, TEST accuracy (0.973) is higher than TRAIN accuracy (0.953). With only 150 test examples, luck moves the score by a couple of percent.
