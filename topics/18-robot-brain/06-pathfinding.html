Finding the way: BFS and A*

The robot needs the shortest route that doesn't go through walls, and a new one when something gets in the way.

Week 5 of 16. So far your robot: ✓ says hello ✓ moves ✓ senses walls ✓ reads real phone data ✓ decides to stop ✓ is tested safe. Today it learns to plan the shortest route around things. Start here

Drop a stone in a pond. The ripple reaches everything 1 step away, then 2 steps, and flows round walls by itself. The first ring to touch the goal tells you the shortest route. A* tilts the pond toward the goal.

Queue
A waiting line: first in, first out.
BFS
Breadth-first search: explore 1 step away, then 2, then 3. Always finds a shortest route.
Heuristic
A quick guess of the steps left, ignoring walls.
A*
Search that tries the square with the smallest "steps so far + guess left" first.

1. The idea

💡 The idea: why not just head for the goal?

"Always step closer to the goal" fails as soon as a wall is in the way. Here the robot steps east, then south, and gets stuck at (2, 2): the wall is right in front of it. Every other move takes it further from the goal (red ring), so it is stuck for ever. The real route goes down and round, away from the goal for a while.

💡 The idea: the ripple (BFS)

Write 0 on the robot's square. Write 1 on its free neighbours, 2 on theirs, and so on. This is the real room from world.py, numbered:

The grey edges show x along the top and y down the side. The bin got 11, so the shortest route is 11 steps. Square (4, 3) (red ring) says 7, though it's only 3 squares right of the start: the ripple went round the wall.

A queue does the numbering, like a line at a shop: take the square at the front, put its new neighbours (blue) at the back.

Every new square also remembers who found it: (1, 2) and (2, 1) were found by (1, 1). That memory is came_from. At the goal, follow it backwards, square by square, to get the route.

💡 The idea: aim the ripple (A*)

BFS spreads in every direction, even away from the bin. A* gives each square a score: steps taken so far + a guess of the steps left. The guess just counts squares across and down, ignoring walls. A* always explores the smallest score next. Here both start at (4, 1) and find the same 8-step route (shaded = explored):

The guess is never too high, so A*'s route is still the shortest. From the corner (1, 1) the gap is smaller, 40 against 35: the bin is in the opposite corner, so almost every square is on some shortest route.

5. Real-world challenge: route-plan your home

🌍 Real-world challenge
  1. On squared paper, draw two or three rooms of your home (one square ≈ one big step). Shade furniture. Pick a start and a goal, like your bed and the fridge.
  2. Run BFS in pencil: 0 on the start, 1 on its neighbours, and so on until you reach the goal. Read the route backwards.
  3. On a fresh copy, run A*: write g + h and always continue from the smallest. Which numbered fewer squares?
  4. Walk the route counting steps. Then put a bag in the way and replan from where you stand.

On your own: this already works alone. If you can't walk around, "walk" the route with a coin on the map.

Check yourself

Why does BFS always find a shortest route? It finishes every square at distance 1 before any at distance 2. So the first time it reaches the goal, no shorter way exists.

bfs_path(ROOM, (1, 1), (1, 1))? [(1, 1)]: zero steps.

Why must the guess never be too big? A* might put off the square on the best route because it looked too expensive, and finish with a longer route.

path_to_commands([(3, 3), (3, 2), (2, 2)])? "NW".

Want to know more?

BFS vs A* in numbers. The room has 42 free squares. BFS takes 40 off the queue before the bin; only (8, 5) and (8, 6) are left. A* takes 35: the robot and the bin are in opposite corners, so almost every square is on some 11-step route. Start at (4, 1) instead and BFS takes 35 while A* takes 13. A* helps most when the goal is in a clear direction.

Common slip: queue.pop() takes from the back, which turns BFS into depth-first search: no promise of the shortest route.

Stretch: use h = 0 in A*: how many squares now? Try a cheating h = 3 × manhattan: is the route always 11?
