Reward
Points after a move: −1 per step, +10 for reaching the goal.
Episode
One attempt, from start to goal (or 200 steps).
Q-value
"How many points do I expect if I make this move here?"
Explore / exploit
Try a random move, or do the best one you know.

1. The idea

💡 The idea: good news travels backwards

In week 5, A* was handed the whole map. Now the robot can't see the maze. It only knows its square, its four moves, and a beep at the goal. So it keeps a score for every square and move, all starting at 0.

When a move reaches the goal, that move scores 10. The square before learns from that square, and so on back (γ = 0.95):

Each move's score = its reward (−1 for a step) + 0.95 × the best score at the square it lands on:

SquareMoveSumScore
(4, 3)E−1 + 0.95 × 108.5
(3, 3)E−1 + 0.95 × 8.57.075
(3, 2)S−1 + 0.95 × 7.075≈ 5.72

Squares further away score less, because there's more walking left. Following the higher score always leads closer to the goal. The −1 per step means "most points" and "shortest route" are the same thing.

So every square keeps four scores, one per move. This is the real square (3, 3) after training. East is best:

In code that one number is Q[((3, 3), "E")], which is 7.07: the key is (square, move).

💡 The idea: don't trust one try, and keep exploring

Go halfway. Early guesses are often wrong, so move the old score only part of the way to the new target, with α = 0.5: a score that keeps getting target 10 goes 0 → 5 → 7.5 → 8.75.

Explore sometimes. If it always picks its best move, it may lock onto the first route that worked. So 20% of the time (ε = 0.2) it picks a random move instead, like trying a new dish instead of your favourite.

5. Real-world challenge: be the Q-learner

🌍 Real-world challenge
  1. Someone draws a secret 4 × 4 maze and hides it. You draw an empty 4 × 4 grid with room for four numbers per square (N, S, E, W), all 0.
  2. Say a move. Your partner answers only "wall" (stay put), "step" (−1) or "goal!" (+10).
  3. Update that square's number: target = reward + 0.9 × (best number where you landed). Use α = 1 (just write the target in).
  4. Play 6 to 8 episodes. Before each move roll a die: on a 1, pick a random direction. Chart your steps per episode.

On your own: draw the maze yourself, fold it face down, and unfold just one square after each move.

Check yourself

Why −1 per step instead of 0? So shorter routes earn more points. The reward says what you want; learning finds how.

A* or Q-learning? A* needs the map and plans instantly. Q-learning needs no map but many tries. If you have a map, use A*.

Q = {((3, 3), "E"): 6.0, ((3, 3), "S"): 6.0}: what does best_action(Q, (3, 3)) return? "S": it ties with E and comes first in "NSEW".

Want to know more?

ε = 0 still works here. With no exploring, the first episodes take 79, 43, 48, 25, 30 steps, then it settles on 7. Every tried move drops below 0, so untried moves (still 0) look best: it explores by accident. In bigger worlds that trick fails, which is why real systems keep some ε.

ε = 1 also finds 7. Moving at random, it still learns about the best moves, because its target always uses the best Q at the next square.

Random wandering alone took 58 steps, then 101, and never improved: it remembered nothing.

Stretch: make the step reward 0: does it still find 7? Start ε at 1.0 and multiply by 0.98 each episode. Draw a bigger MAZE.
