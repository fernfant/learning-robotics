Words as numbers: a tiny next-word model

Count which word follows which, turn the counts into chances, and roll a die. That's a language model, the great-great-grandparent of ChatGPT. Then your robot uses it to fix typos.

Start here

A computer can "write" by doing one thing over and over: look at the last word, guess what usually comes next, pick one, repeat.

Your phone keyboard does this. Type "see you" and it offers "tomorrow", "soon", "later". It isn't thinking. It has counted which words you usually type after "you". The same counting fixes typos: "teh" becomes "the" because "the" is a real word one slip away, and it fits after the word before.

Token
One piece of text: for us, a word or a full stop.
Bigram
Two tokens side by side, like ("the", "robot").
Probability
A chance from 0 (never) to 1 (always). 0.25 means 1 time in 4.
Sampling
Picking at random, where likelier options come up more often.
Edit
One typo: a letter deleted, two swapped, one replaced or one added.

1. The idea

💡 The idea

Our model reads one short story, the STORY in your homework file ("the robot picks up the red block . the robot puts the red block in the bin . …", 66 tokens). Then it keeps a tally: after this word, which word came next? Here's every word that ever came after "robot":

Thicker arrow = seen more often. Divide each count by the total (8) and you get probabilities that add up to 1. To write, roll a weighted die: "picks" comes up 1 time in 4. Add the word, then look at its arrows, and repeat. That's the whole model.

Real output from the solution (seed 4, 12 new words each):

-> the robot picks up the robot puts the cat walks away . the
-> the robot picks up the blue block in the robot stops . the
-> the blue block in the red block . the blue block in the

Every pair of neighbours was in the story, but the sentences wander. Our model remembers only one word. After "the" it has no idea it was in the middle of "picks up the …". ChatGPT runs the same loop (pick the next token, add it, repeat), but it looks back over thousands of tokens.

💡 The idea: fixing typos

Step 1: list every string one typo away and keep only real words the model knows. Step 2: let the word before choose. The known words come from the robot's 34 example commands (from Part B). The same typo, "te", gets two different fixes:

If no candidate was ever seen after the word before, pick the most common one. If there are no candidates at all, leave the word alone.

5. Real-world challenge: a language model in a cup

🌍 Real-world challenge
  1. Copy about 20 messages you sent onto paper (leave out anything private).
  2. Pick your most common word, say "i". Tally the words that came right after it.
  3. Write each follower on scraps of paper, one scrap per tally mark, and put them in a cup. The cup is the probabilities.
  4. Make cups for a few more common words. Draw a scrap, write it down, use that word's cup next. Stop at a word with no cup. Make 3 "sentences".

On your own: the same game works with any page of your own writing. Then paste it into the first demo and compare. Record one probability you worked out, like P(am after i) = 3/4. The lesson: the model is just your habits, counted. It can sound like you without knowing what it says.

6. Check yourself

What does a bigram model remember when it picks the next word?

Only the last word. That's why its sentences wander.

Predict: what does tokenize("STOP! Stop, now.") return?

["stop", "stop", "now", "."].

Can the model ever write "the robot sees a block"?

No. In the story "a" is only ever followed by "cat". A bigram model can make new sentences, but never a pair it hasn't seen.

Why sample at random instead of always taking the top word?

Always taking the top word gives the same sentence every time and often loops. Sampling gives variety while still favouring likely words.

Why does "te" become "to" after "go" but "the" after "put"?

Both are one typo away. The fixer keeps the one most likely after the word before.

7. Want to know more?

Sampling: how to roll a weighted die

Picture a ruler from 0 to 1, cut into pieces as long as each probability. After "the" (robot 0.5, red 0.125, bin 0.125, cat 0.0625, blue 0.125, room 0.0625):

0             0.5    0.625  0.75  0.8125 0.9375  1
|    robot     | red  | bin  | cat |  blue | room |

Pick a random number and see where it lands. random.Random(4) gives 0.236 first, so "robot". rng.choices does this for you.

How is ChatGPT different?
  • Longer memory: thousands of earlier tokens, not one word.
  • A neural network instead of a count table. Counting "last 10 words → next" with 50,000 words would need 50,00011 slots, almost all zero. A network learns patterns instead. You build a small one in week 8.
  • Far more text: trillions of tokens, not 66.
  • Word pieces: "unboxable" becomes "un" + "box" + "able".
  • Extra training to follow instructions and refuse harmful requests.
More about the typo fixer

A 3-letter word has 3 deletes, 2 swaps, 78 replaces and 104 inserts: 187, or 182 different strings. Looking two typos away would fix "bclok", but "bclok" has 286 one-typo strings and 36,860 two-typo ones, so there are many more chances of a wrong guess.

"drop it in the bn" becomes drop is in the bin. "it" isn't in the 34 commands, and "is" is one letter away, so the fixer "fixes" a correct word. A phone has a full dictionary; ours only knows what it counted.

Measured with python3 progress.py --solutions: typo commands handled exactly like the correct spelling go from 6/9 to 9/9 for the word counter.
