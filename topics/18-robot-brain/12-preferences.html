Start here

Can't write down what "good" means? Ask people to compare two options. Learn a score that agrees with their choices. Then pick whatever scores highest.

At the optician they never ask "how sharp is this, out of 10?". They ask "better with lens one… or lens two?", again and again, and work out a precise prescription. We'll do the same with routes.

Feature
A number describing an option. Each route gets three: length, how many squares brush a wall, how many turns.
Reward model
A scorer that gives liked options higher scores.
Best-of-n
Score all the options and pick the top one.
RLHF
Reinforcement learning from human feedback: learning from people's choices. It's how chatbots learn to be helpful.

1. The idea

💡 The idea: which route do you like?

Both routes go from the start (1, 1) to the bin (7, 6). Both are 11 steps. A* from week 5 only knows length, so it can't tell them apart, and happens to give you P.

Most people pick Q. Can you write the rule? "Each step costs 1, each scrape 0.5, each turn 1"… where did 0.5 come from? You made it up. But "A or B?" takes everyone one second. So we collect lots of "A or B?" answers and let maths find the taste.

How it works

💡 The idea · a route is three numbers

Count three things on a route: its length (moves), how many squares brush a wall, and how many turns. Route Q is (11, 8, 1): 11 moves, the 8 ringed squares touch a wall, and it turns once.

A reward is week 4's neuron: multiply each number by a taste weight and add up. The simulated person's secret taste is (−1.0, −0.6, −0.8), so route P scores −19.4 and Q scores −16.6. Higher wins: Q.

💡 The idea · people are a bit random

Even when Q scores higher, people sometimes pick P. The bigger the gap between the scores, the surer they are. This S-shaped curve (the sigmoid) turns a gap into a chance:

Then we run it backwards: from 150 real answers, find the weights that make those answers most likely. That's the training step (step 4).

Predict first: count route Q's near_wall by hand

8. The six squares along the top touch the top wall (6), (7, 4) has a wall to its west at (6, 4) (the sneaky one), and (7, 6) has the bottom wall (1). Each square counts once, even if it touches two walls.

🛠 Build
  1. route_features(room, path) → (length, near_wall, turns). Length is moves, not squares. near_wall counts squares after the start with a wall N, S, E or W. Example: route_features(ROOM, [(1, 1), (2, 1), (3, 1)]) → (2, 2, 0).

🛠 Build
  1. train_reward_model takes comparisons (defaults lr=0.01, epochs=300): start at [0, 0, 0]; for each epoch, for each (fa, fb, chose_a), compute p once, then nudge all three weights: w[i] += lr × (chose_a − p) × (A[i] − B[i]). A surprise (big chose_a − p) means a big nudge. With the single comparison ((11, 8, 1), (11, 8, 3), 1) and epochs=1 you get [0, 0, -0.01].
  2. agreement(weights, comparisons): the fraction where the model's favourite is the one the person chose.
  3. best_route(weights, room, routes): best-of-n, the route with the highest reward.

5. Real-world challenge

🌍 Real-world challenge · learn a real person's taste
  1. Pick 8–10 options with three numbers each: walking routes (minutes, busy roads, prettiness 0–3) or snacks (price, sweetness 0–5, healthiness 0–5).
  2. Ask a family member "this one or this one?" for 20 random pairs, without showing the numbers. Record A, B and 1 or 0. Sneak in one pair twice.
  3. Find their weights: one pass by hand with lr = 0.1, or type the answers into your train_reward_model.
  4. Predict 5 brand-new pairs, then ask. How many did you get right? Tell them their "taste weights".

On your own (15 minutes). Be your own person: give 8 snacks three numbers, compare 15 pairs without looking at the numbers, and train on your answers. Does the result match what you think you care about?

6. Check yourself

Why "A or B?" instead of "score it out of 10"?

People compare much more consistently. One person's 7 is another's 9, and the same person drifts day to day.

Predict: prob_prefers_a([-1, 0, 0], (5, 0, 0), (9, 0, 0))?

Rewards −5 and −9, gap 4, sigmoid(4) ≈ 0.98.

All your training routes are 11 steps. What length weight do you learn?

Exactly 0.0: every length difference is 0, so that weight is always nudged by 0. A model can only learn about things that vary in its data. (That's why candidate_routes includes some silly 13-step detours.)

How is the reward here different from the bonus maze lesson's?

There we wrote the reward by hand (−1 per step, +10 at the goal). Here it's learned from people. RLHF = learn the reward from humans + reinforcement learning on it.

7. Want to know more?

The learning rule, by hand 
Comparison: A = (11, 8, 1)  B = (11, 8, 3)  person chose A (chose_a = 1)
  weights now      [0, 0, 0]
  p                = sigmoid(0 − 0) = 0.5
  chose_a − p      = 0.5
  A − B            = (0, 0, −2)
  change           = 0.01 × 0.5 × (0, 0, −2) = (0, 0, −0.01)
  weights after    [0, 0, −0.01]       ← "turns are bad", learned!

Sometimes a person picks the longer route (people are a bit random). One answer pushes the weight the wrong way, but over 150 comparisons the sensible ones outvote it, like averaging noisy readings in week 2. With only 10 simulated answers in the demo, the near_wall weight even comes out positive (+0.96): small data fools models.

How real chatbots use this (RLHF, PPO, reward hacking)

Big assistants use the same recipe, scaled up: a model writes two answers, a person picks one, hundreds of thousands of times; a big neural network learns to score answers with the same sigmoid-of-the-gap rule; then, instead of best-of-n, reinforcement learning (PPO or GRPO) changes the model itself to write high-scoring answers first time. The catch is reward hacking: pushed hard, the model finds weird outputs the imperfect reward model loves. A KL penalty is the leash: "get more reward, but stay close to your old self".
