Start here

Sometimes a big model needs only a small change. Don't rewrite it. Freeze it, and train a thin add-on that holds just the change.

Robo-Helper has six distance sensors. The factory model turns their readings into six "danger levels". On your robot, sensor 2 is bolted on at an angle, so its readings leak into every danger level. Tested 200 times, the factory model's error is 0.0673: too much for a robot near a cat.

Think of glasses off the shelf that are nearly right. You don't grind new lenses. You add a thin clip-on that fixes just the difference, and you can unclip it any time.

Training an already-trained model a bit more, for a new job.

Low-Rank Adaptation: fix a frozen model with a small trained patch.

1. The idea

💡 A model is a table

To get the outputs, take each row of the table, multiply it number by number with the inputs, and add up. That's matrix × vector, and it's week 4's neuron done for several neurons at once.

Fine-tuning nudges every number in W, like week 2's line fitting. Fine for 36 numbers. But one layer of a big language model is about 4096 × 4096 = 16,777,216 numbers, and there are hundreds of layers. And every robot would need its own giant copy.

💡 LoRA: a small patch on a big table

Look at what your robot actually needs to change. Sensor 2 leaks into each danger level by [0.5, −0.3, 0.8, 0, −0.4, 0.2]. That fills just one column of a 6 × 6 change table. 36 numbers, but only one idea. So write it as a thin column A ("how much each danger level is affected") times a thin row B ("which sensor causes it"):

The model's output becomes W x + A (B x). W stays frozen; only A and B train. B starts at zero, so the patch starts empty and the model starts as exactly the factory model.

At real size the patch is tiny. A 4096 × 4096 layer has 16,777,216 numbers. A rank-8 patch (A is 4096 × 8, B is 8 × 4096) trains 65,536 of them, about 0.4%:

That "8" is the rank: how many separate root causes the patch can fix. One crooked sensor is one cause. Two crooked sensors need rank 2.

💡 The idea · passing the blame back

How do A and B learn? Think of a relay race that ends wrong. The judge at the finish tells the last runner, A, how far off the answer was (the error). A fixes its own part, and also passes a message back to the first runner, B: "this much of the blame is yours". That message is called back.

Work out back before you change A, so B hears the message from the old A. You saw this in the Play demo: each "Train one step" does exactly this for all 12 examples.

🛠 Build
  1. train_lora(W, examples, rank=1, lr=0.05, steps=400, rng=None): keep W frozen. A is 6 × rank small random numbers, B is rank × 6 zeros. For each step and each example, nudge A and B. The docstring has the exact rule. Then run the file: python3 lesson13_lora.py.

5. Real-world challenge: adapt, don't rebuild

🌍 Real-world challenge

A kitchen scale. Weigh 4 or 5 things whose true weight you know (a sealed 500 g packet, a 1 kg bag of sugar). Is the scale off by the same amount every time, or the same percentage? Write that one correction on a sticky note instead of re-marking every line.

On your own (15 minutes): measure 5 things with a ruler, but start every measurement at the 1 cm mark. Then measure them properly from 0. Five wrong numbers, one root cause, one fix: "take away 1". That's rank 1.

One root cause can change many numbers and still be one small rule. Most real fixes are like that, which is why LoRA works so well.

6. Check yourself

matvec([[0, 1], [1, 0]], [5, 9]): what comes out, and what does this table do?

[9, 5]. It swaps the two inputs.

How many numbers does a rank-2 LoRA on a 6 × 6 table train?

A is 6 × 2 = 12 and B is 2 × 6 = 12, so 24. Still fewer than 36.

Why does B start at zero? What if A and B both did?

B at zero makes the patch empty, so the model starts as exactly the factory model. If both were zero, every nudge would be multiplied by zero, and nothing would ever learn.

Is LoRA more accurate than full fine-tuning?

Not here: both reach 0.0000. Its wins are far fewer numbers to train and store, the original model left untouched, and small adapters you can swap per robot.

Want to know more?

Fine-tuning by hand

The rule is err = W x − y, then W[i][j] −= lr × err[i] × x[j]. With W = [[1, 0], [0, 1]], x = [1, 0.5], y = [1, 1], lr 0.5: err = [0, −0.5], so W becomes [[1, 0], [0.25, 1.125]] and W x = [1, 0.8125], closer to [1, 1].

What LoRA found inside

After training, A came out as about [0.48, −0.29, 0.77, 0, −0.38, 0.19] and B as about [0.01, 0, 1.05, 0, 0, 0]. B points at sensor 2, and A × 1.05 gives back the leak. Nobody told it which sensor was bent.

Why robotics people love this

Robot foundation models like OpenVLA take camera images and an instruction and output actions. They are commonly fine-tuned with LoRA for a new robot or task: one shared frozen model, plus a small adapter per robot. Servers can even switch adapters request by request ("multi-LoRA serving"). On a real robot (see week 9's wheels and week 16's go-real page), "measure a few examples and fit a small correction" is often the first thing you do.
