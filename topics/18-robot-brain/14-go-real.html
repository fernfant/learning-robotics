Start here

A project nobody can understand might as well not exist, and a simulation is only a rehearsal for the real, messy world.

The problem

You've built something genuinely impressive: a robot that understands commands, plans routes, brakes safely, dodges a cat and grades itself. Right now it lives in a folder only you know about. If you showed it to your teacher, your friends or the judges at a science fair, could they run it in five minutes? Would they know what it gets wrong? And the room it drives around is made of text characters. A real floor has slippery tiles, socks and an actual cat.

The idea in one picture

Think of a science fair. The best stands aren't the ones that claim "it works perfectly". They're the ones with a clear poster, a working demo, a graph of results, and a corner that says "what went wrong and what we learned". The judges trust those stands, because they're honest. A README is your science-fair poster. A real robot is taking your experiment out of the lab.

Words to know

README
The front-page text file of a project. It says what it is, how to run it, and how well it works.
Demo
A short recording or live show of the project doing its thing, failures included.
Showcase
A place where you show off your projects, like a science-fair table or a web page.
Ultrasonic sensor
A sensor that measures distance by sending a sound too high to hear and timing the echo, like a bat.
Drift
When a real robot slowly ends up somewhere other than where it thinks it is, because wheels slip.
Kill switch
A physical switch that cuts the power instantly, whatever the code is doing.

The full lesson: the boss gap first (in Add it to your robot), then four parts: showing your work, going physical (optional), safety and ethics, and where to go next. The only Python is the boss gap. The rest of your homework is a README and a demo.

1. The problem

Two problems, actually.

Nobody can see your work. Your code is spread over 20 files. Someone new doesn't know to start at my_robot.py, doesn't know about --show or check.py, and doesn't know what your robot is bad at. Without a guide, most people give up after a minute.

The pretend world is too kind. In world.py, a step east moves the robot exactly one square. The sensor in week 2 has neat, made-up noise. The cat sits still on one square. Real motors don't turn exactly the same amount each time, real sensors get confused by soft cushions, and real cats move. Every robot that works in real life first worked in a simulation, and then had to be fixed for the real world.

2. First principles

Part A: showing your work

Start from the reader. Someone opens your project. In order, they want to know:

  1. What is it? One sentence, plus a picture or short recording.
  2. Can I run it? The exact commands, copy-pasteable.
  3. How does it work? The pipeline, in a small diagram. Which part does what.
  4. How well does it work? Real numbers from a real test, and what the test was.
  5. What fails, and why? The honest list.
  6. What would you do next?

That list is your README's outline. You have every piece already, and the numbers come from your robot. Run python3 my_robot.py --show (section 6) and copy two results: the exam line (I passed 12 of 12. once you've beaten the boss) and your own tests' line (for example Zippy passed 6 of 8 of your tests.), with its ✗ lines. Add week 11's bake-off table, too: which brain passed what, how long it thought, and what it cost. Your robot's story is in there: 5/12 with week 5's word counter, 8/12 with week 6's tiny LM, 10/12 with week 9's teacher, and 12/12 with the boss. (Week 7's capstone RoboHelper, with its 83%, was the careful test version of the same robot. You can mention it as where the testing started.)

Predict first: two READMEs. A says "My robot understands commands perfectly! 12 out of 12!". B says "12 of 12 on the course exam, but 6 of 8 on my own harder tests. Say 'nearest' instead of 'closest' and it bins the wrong block, because my boss rule only knows the word 'closest'." Which one would an engineer trust more?

B, by a mile. "Perfectly" makes an engineer suspicious straight away, because nothing is perfect, so either you didn't test it or you're hiding something. B shows that you tested (even beyond the exam), you measured, you understood the failure, and you can explain it. That's exactly what people who hire engineers look for. Honest failures are a strength, not a weakness.

See how grown-up AI work reports results: the "reading the results honestly" section of the distillation module is a good model, with numbers next to what they do and don't prove. And the open-source portfolio stage shows how grown-up engineers share projects like yours.

The demo recording. 30 to 60 seconds is plenty. Most computers have a built-in screen recorder. The easiest way: record python3 my_robot.py --show. Show: one command working, the cat stop and replan, the 12 of 12 exam, and one failure on purpose from your own tests. Talk over it, or add captions. Then put the recording file (or a GIF) next to your README and link to it.

Part B: going physical (optional)

You don't need hardware to finish this course. But if you want to see your ideas move, a cheap wheeled robot kit is the next step. Look for one with two motors and an ultrasonic distance sensor. Kits built around a micro:bit (which can run MicroPython, a small version of Python) or an Arduino (usually programmed in C++, which looks different but uses the same ideas) are common, well documented, and used in lots of schools.

How an ultrasonic sensor works, from first principles. It sends a short click of sound, too high-pitched to hear, and times how long the echo takes to come back. Sound travels at about 343 metres per second in air. The sound goes there and back, so you halve it:

echo time   = 2.9 milliseconds = 0.0029 s
distance    = 343 × 0.0029 ÷ 2
            = 0.497 m   ≈ half a metre      (one Robo-Helper square!)

Now map each lesson onto the real robot:

WeekIn the simulationOn a real robot
Week 2: sensorsnoisy_reading, averageTake 5 to 10 ultrasonic readings in a row and average them. Real readings jump around, especially off soft or angled things. Week 2's maths is exactly why averaging helps. (And if your sensor is mounted at an angle, week 9's LoRA idea is how you'd correct it.)
Week 2: brakingSTOP_DATA, fit_line, max_safe_speedDrive at a few motor power levels, cut the power, and measure with a tape how far it rolls. That's your own STOP_DATA. Fit the line on a laptop, then put the two numbers (w and b) into the robot's code.
Week 3: stop decisionshould_stop, safest_thresholdInputs: smoothed distance and current speed. Start with a simple safe rule, then collect real examples ("should it have stopped?") and train the neuron. Choose a threshold that favours recall: braking for nothing is fine, hitting the wall is not.
Week 4: pathfindingastar_path, path_to_commandsUse floor tiles (or tape squares on the floor) as your grid. Draw the map, plan with A*, and turn the path into moves: "forward one tile, turn left, forward two tiles".

The big surprise: drift. Tell a real robot "forward one tile" ten times and it won't land exactly ten tiles away, slightly to the left. Wheels slip and motors differ. This is why real robots keep sensing while they move, and correct themselves, instead of trusting the plan blindly. It's the same lesson as week 7, in motors instead of words: never trust, always check.

Predict first: your robot plans a 6-tile route along a corridor using only "forward one tile" commands. What's the most likely thing to go wrong?

It drifts sideways and bumps a wall, or stops a bit short. Each move is slightly off, and the errors add up over 6 moves. The fix is feedback: use the distance sensor to check the gap to the wall ahead, and stop based on what it measures, not on how many moves you planned.

Later: a robot arm. When you're ready for a bigger project, look at LeRobot, Hugging Face's open-source robotics library. It supports low-cost robot arms that people build themselves, and lets you teach an arm by showing it what to do, then training a neural network on your demonstrations. Their free robotics course is a good next step after this one. This is a project for later, with an adult: it needs more money, tools and patience.

Part C: safety and ethics

Moving robots can hurt people and pets. Even a small one can pinch fingers in its wheels, trip someone, scare a pet, or drive off a table or down the stairs. Follow these rules every time:

  • Always have a physical off switch you can reach instantly. Not a button in your code: a real switch that cuts the power, because when the code is the problem, the code can't save you.
  • Test with the wheels off the ground first (prop it on a book), then on the floor at the slowest speed, away from stairs, table edges, pets and little brothers or sisters.
  • Adult supervision for anything with tools (soldering irons, hot glue guns, craft knives) and for batteries. Rechargeable lithium batteries can get very hot or catch fire if they're squashed, short-circuited or charged the wrong way. Use only the batteries and charger the kit says, and never leave them charging unattended.

Ethics, in plain words. Week 7's rule still applies: never let AI output control something that moves without checks you wrote. If your robot ever has a camera or microphone, ask people before recording them. And be honest in what you claim, in your README and in conversation. "It passed 6 of my 8 tests" is a true sentence. "It understands language" is not.

Add it to your robot: the BOSS


Since week 11 your robot's exam has ended the same way:


  ✗ 'tidy up the room'                             binned ['green block'], wanted ['blue block', 'green block', 'red block']
  ✗ "put away the block that's closest to the bin" binned nothing, wanted ['blue block']
  I passed 10 of 12.


Every brain in the bake-off failed these two. A word-reading brain only sees words. It has never heard "room", and "closest" is about the room, not the words. More training can't fix that. But your robot isn't only a word-reader. It knows where every block is (self.objects), which ones are already binned (self.binned), and where the bin is (find_bin(ROOM)). It doesn't need to understand "closest". It can measure it.



Work it out by hand first


The blocks start at red (2, 1), blue (6, 3) and green (1, 5). The bin is at (7, 6). Week 1's manhattan(a, b) is the grid distance: |x1 − x2| + |y1 − y2|.


Predict first: which block is closest to the bin?

red    (2, 1) → (7, 6):  |2 − 7| + |1 − 6| = 5 + 5 = 10
blue   (6, 3) → (7, 6):  |6 − 7| + |3 − 6| = 1 + 3 =  4   ← smallest
green  (1, 5) → (7, 6):  |1 − 7| + |5 − 6| = 6 + 1 =  7


Blue, at 4 squares. That's exactly what the exam wants: wanted ['blue block'].


Predict first: a rule says "if the command contains everything, put every block away". What happens to exam task 6, "bin everything except the green block"?

It bins all three, including green: a fail! Task 6 passes today, and your new rule would break it. That's why the rule has a "but not if" part: if the command says except or but, leave it to your language brain, which already gets task 6 right. A new rule must never break an old pass. That's why you re-run the whole exam, not just the new tasks.



Fill in see_and_plan(self, text)


Read its docstring in my_robot.py. It returns a plan (a list of actions, like your week 6 brain writes), or None:


    

  • the command has the word closest → put away the unbinned block nearest the bin;
    • 

  • the command has room, everything or all, but not except or but → put away every unbinned block, red, then blue, then green;
    • 

  • anything else → None, and your language brain handles it as usual.
    • 



An action looks like {"intent": "put", "object": "blue block"}. The provided understand() asks see_and_plan first. If it returns a plan, the robot uses it. If it returns None, the language brain takes over.


Hint 1
Start the method with this import line:
from lesson01_moving import manhattan
find_bin and ROOM are already imported at the top of my_robot.py.


Hint 2
First make two lists. The words: words = text.lower().split(). The blocks still on the floor: left = each of "red block", "blue block", "green block" that isn't in self.binned. Then three ifs, in the order of the bullets above. For "closest", min with a key finds the block whose distance to the bin is smallest. Check that left isn't empty first.


Hint 3 (fill in the blanks)
words = text.lower().split()
left = [b for b in ("red block", "blue block", "green block") if b not in self.binned]
if "closest" in words and left:
    nearest = min(left, key=lambda b: manhattan(self.objects[b], ____))
    return [{"intent": "put", "object": ____}]

    return [{"intent": "put", "object": b} for b in ____]
return None


New Python this time. min(things, key=...) finds the thing with the smallest score, and lambda b: ... is a tiny one-line function that works out each score. any(...) is True if at least one check is True.

min(["cat", "elephant", "ox"], key=len)              →  "ox"
min([5, -9, 2], key=lambda n: abs(n))                →  2
any(w in ["hi", "there"] for w in ("hello", "hi"))   →  True

7. Common mistakes


    

  • Hiding the failures. A README that only shows the good bits reads like an advert. Show the 12 of 12 and the ✗ lines from your own tests.
    • 

  • A boss rule that breaks an old pass. If see_and_plan grabs "everything" without checking for "except", exam task 6 bins the green block. Always re-run the whole exam. check.py: "'bin everything except the green block' says except: leave it to the language brain (return None)".
    • 

  • "How to run" that doesn't run. Test the commands from a fresh terminal, in a fresh copy of the folder.
    • 

  • A ten-minute demo. Nobody watches it. Under a minute, and the best part first.
    • 

  • Testing a real robot on a table. It will drive off the edge one day. Floor only, slow first, off switch in reach.
    • 

  • Trusting the plan on real wheels. Drift adds up. Keep sensing while moving.

8. Check yourself


Name the six questions a README should answer.
What is it? Can I run it? How does it work? How well does it work? What fails and why? What would you do next?


An ultrasonic echo comes back after 5.8 ms. How far away is the object?
343 × 0.0058 ÷ 2 ≈ 0.99 m. Twice the time, twice the distance: about one metre.


Predict: your real robot plans "forward 4 tiles" and you measure where it stops, five times. Will all five be the same?
No. They'll spread out a bit, and probably all lean slightly one way. That's drift plus noise, the real-world version of week 2. Averaging the five shows the typical error, and checking with the sensor while driving fixes it.


Why must the off switch be physical, not a button in your code?
If the code has a bug or freezes, a software button may not work. A physical switch cuts the power whatever the code is doing.


Which week's idea is "keep sensing while you move, don't trust the plan blindly" most like?
Week 7: never trust, always check. There it was AI output, here it's your own motion plan. Your robot's stop-and-replan around the cat (weeks 3 and 4) is the same idea too.


Why could see_and_plan beat the two boss tasks when no amount of training on words could?
Because it uses what the robot can see: where every block is and where the bin is. "Closest" is a fact about the room, so you measure it with manhattan instead of guessing from words. The language brains only ever saw the sentence.
