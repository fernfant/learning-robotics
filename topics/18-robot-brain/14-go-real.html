1. The boss: use your eyes, not just words

💡 The idea

Since week 15 the exam has failed "tidy up the room" and "the block closest to the bin". A word-reading brain has never seen "room", and "closest" isn't in the sentence at all: it's in the room. But your robot isn't only a word-reader. It knows where every block is and where the bin is. It doesn't need to understand "closest". It can measure it.

This is a hybrid: a simple fast rule for what the robot can measure, and the learned brain for everything else. Real robots are built like that.

Predict first: a rule says "if the command contains everything, bin every block". What happens to exam task 6, "bin everything except the green block"?

It bins all three, including green: a fail! Task 6 passes today. That's why the rule has a "but not if" part: if the command says except or but, leave it to the language brain. A new rule must never break an old pass, so you always re-run the whole exam.

🛠 Build: see_and_plan(self, text)

It returns a plan (a list of actions like {"intent": "put", "object": "blue block"}) or None. The provided understand() asks it first; None means the language brain takes over.

  1. Make two lists: the words (text.lower().split()) and left, the blocks not yet in self.binned.
  2. If the words include closest → put away the block in left nearest the bin.
  3. If they include room, everything or all, but not except or but → put away every block in left, red, then blue, then green.
  4. Anything else → return None.

2. The body capstone: the rescue

💡 The idea

Someone picks your robot up and puts it down somewhere. It has no idea where. Your job: rescue blue. It must find itself, plan a route, and drive there on real(istic) wheels that slip. Every robotics week you did is one link in the chain:

Solid boxes are functions rescue() calls. Dashed boxes are ideas inside them: the particle filter blends scans and wheel counts the way week 6 blended two sensors, and week 10's "always check" is the final step where you compare with the truth.

🛠 Build: rescue(self, name)
  1. Get lost, then find yourself (week 12): body = Body(start=(6, 3), heading_deg=180, seed=5), then guess = localise(body, random.Random(7)). The guess is (x, y, heading) in metres.
  2. Plan (weeks 11 and 5): start = square_of(guess[0], guess[1]), goal = self.objects[name + " block"], path = astar_path(ROOM, start, goal)[0].
  3. Drive (week 9): odo = Odometer(body, guess), then drive_to(body, odo, centre(sq)) for every square in path[1:].
  4. Check (week 10): x, y, _ = body.true_pose(); miss = the distance from (x, y) to centre(goal). Return f"Rescued the {name} block: I started lost, found myself, and drove there. I'm {miss:.2f} m from it."

✅ Checkpoint 3: the rescue

From homework, run python3 my_robot.py, type reset (so the blue block is back on the floor), then:

Zippy> rescue blue
  Rescued the blue block: I started lost, found myself, and drove there. I'm 0.10 m from it.

Look at the two numbers: its first guess was only 0.04 m off (that's whereami), and it misses by 0.10 m. The extra 6 cm comes from the drive: the wheels slip, and drive_to stops as soon as it thinks it's within 5 cm. Odometry can't correct slip, and it can't fix a wrong start either, because it drives the plan from wherever it believes it started. A good guess is what makes a good rescue. Try it: rescue red and rescue green. What goes wrong?

Red, real output: I'm 1.24 m from it. The planned route to red runs along the top, through (4, 1), and A* uses the official map, which doesn't know about the cat. The body bumps into the cat and gets stuck, while its wheel counting believes it arrived.

Green, real output: I'm 1.20 m from it. No cat this time. The route squeezes round the corner of the wall at (3, 3), the body catches on that corner and sticks there for most of the trip. Its wheels keep turning, so its odometry thinks it carried on along the route and arrived. Really it stopped more than a metre short.

Both are honest failures with the same cause: nothing checked the laser while driving. That's your "what I'd do next": plan on a map that knows about the cat (your week-11 map already found it), and keep scanning while you drive, so the robot notices when it's stuck.

⚠️ If you build a real robot

Moving robots can pinch fingers, trip people, scare pets and drive off tables. Always have a physical off switch, test with the wheels off the ground first, then on the floor at the slowest speed, away from stairs and pets. An adult supervises tools and batteries: use only the kit's batteries and charger, and never leave them charging alone.

7. Check yourself


Why could see_and_plan beat the boss tasks when no amount of training on words could?
"Closest" is a fact about the room, not the sentence. The robot knows where everything is, so it measures with manhattan instead of guessing from words.


Is the boss rule cheating?
No: it's a hybrid, how real robots are built. But a rule only knows the words you gave it: "nearest" doesn't fire it. Say so in your README.


The rescue's first guess was 0.04 m off and it missed by 0.10 m. Why can't driving fix that?
Odometry only counts how far the wheels turned from where the robot believes it started. A wrong start gives a wrong finish, however good the steering, and wheel slip on the way adds a little more (plus up to 5 cm, because drive_to stops when it's "close enough"). Keep sensing while you drive to correct it.


Why did rescue red miss by 1.24 m, and rescue green by 1.20 m?
Red: A* planned on the official map, which doesn't know the cat, and the body bumped into the cat and stuck. Green: the body caught on a wall corner and stuck. Both times the wheels kept turning, so the wheel counting believed it arrived: never trust, always check.


Name the six questions a README should answer.
What is it? How do I run it? How does it work? How well does it work? What fails, and why? What would you do next?



8. Want to know more?


Going physical: a real kit

A cheap kit with two motors and an ultrasonic distance sensor, built around a micro:bit (MicroPython) or an Arduino (C++), is the next step. An ultrasonic sensor times an echo: sound travels about 343 m/s, there and back, so an echo after 2.9 ms means 343 × 0.0029 ÷ 2 ≈ 0.50 m, one square. Map each week onto it: average 5–10 readings (week 2), measure your own braking line with a tape (week 2), train the stop decision (week 4), tape a grid on the floor for A* (week 5), and use the motor encoders, PID and odometry exactly like week 9. Expect drift: that's why weeks 11 and 12 exist.
