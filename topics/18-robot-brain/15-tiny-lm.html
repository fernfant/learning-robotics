Start here

A language model does one thing, over and over: read everything so far, give every possible next word a probability, pick one, and repeat. Ours is small enough to build by hand, and it still does exactly that.

The problem

Your robot's week 5 brain counts words and picks one action. Say "put the red and blue blocks away" and it bins the red block and forgets the blue one. Say "put the block away" and it can't ask "which block?". It can only pick a pile. Real LLMs don't sort into piles. They write their answer, word by word, as long as it needs to be. Can we build one of those ourselves, from nothing, in one Python file?

The idea in one picture

Think of a game of "finish my sentence" with a friend. You say "put the red block in the…" and they say "bin" without thinking. They're not looking anything up. They've heard enough sentences that the next word just feels likely. Now imagine they had to give a percentage for every word they could say: bin 90%, box 6%, sea 1%… That list of percentages is exactly what our model produces at every step. Then it says the top one, adds it to the answer, and goes again, until it says "I'm done".

Words to know

Embedding
A short list of numbers that stands for a word. Ours are 12 numbers long. The model learns them; we never choose them.
Softmax
The recipe that turns any list of scores into probabilities that add up to 1.
Loss (surprise)
How surprised the model was by the right answer. Low probability for the right word = big surprise. Training makes this small.
Parameter
One of the model's learnable numbers. Ours has 2,702. Big LLMs have billions.
More words you'll meet below (look them up when you get there)
Average
Add the lists up position by position, then divide by how many there are. It's how our model "reads" a whole command.
Hidden layer
A row of 32 neurons (like week 3's) sitting between the input and the answer. Nobody tells them what to do; they learn it.
tanh
A squash function. Any number goes in, a number between −1 and 1 comes out. Like week 3's sigmoid, but centred on 0.
Score
A raw number for each possible next word. Bigger means "more likely", but it isn't a probability yet.
Greedy decoding
Writing by always picking the single most likely next word.
Synthetic data
Training examples made by a program instead of written one by one. We fill in sentence templates to make hundreds.
Held-out test
Examples kept away from training, used only to check whether the model learned the idea or just memorised.
Hallucination
A fluent, confident answer that isn't backed by the input. The model makes something up because it must say something.

What this adds to your robot: a brain that writes whole plans. Last week, "put the red and blue blocks away" binned only the red block. After this week both go in the bin, and "put the block away" makes your robot ask "Which block do you mean?". In week 11 your robot sits a 12-task exam (the bake-off): the week 5 word counter passes 5 of the 12 tasks, and this brain passes 8.

1. The problem

Here's what our earlier models can and can't do:

  • Week 5's next-word counts (the ones your typo fixer uses) look at one previous word. It can write, but it forgets everything except the last word. It could never read a seven-word command and answer it.
  • Week 5's word counter reads the whole command, but treats every word as a separate clue. "halt" and "freeze" are as different to it as "halt" and "bin". And it picks one of four piles, so every answer is exactly one action.

One action isn't enough for a tidying robot. Real output from the week 11 exam: for "put the red and blue blocks away" the word counter replies The red block is in the bin. and stops. For "bin everything except the green block" it bins the green block, the one you said to leave. For "put the block away" it replies Sorry, I can't do that: 'put' needs an object instead of asking which block.

We want one model that reads the whole command and writes a plan, however long:

"put the red and blue blocks away"  →  put  red  blue  <end>
"clean up everything"               →  put  red  blue  green  <end>
"everything except green"           →  put  red  blue  <end>
"put the block away"                →  ask  <end>            (which block? it asks you)
"make me a cup of tea"              →  none  <end>           (not a robot job)
"halt"                              →  stop  <end>

The model writes from a menu of 10 words: put go find stop ask none red blue green <end>. The <end> word means "I've finished". The longest plan, put red blue green <end>, is 5 words, so it never writes more than MAX_WORDS = 5. This is the same job as week 5's next-word model (predict the next word, repeat) but now the prediction depends on the whole command too.

Why not just one action? We measured that too. A model like this one that writes only one action, like put red <end>, is a cleverer pile-sorter, nothing more. On the exam it scores 4/12: worse than the word counter's 5/12. A brain that can write should write what the job needs.

2. First principles

Step 1: give every word a list of numbers

Week 5's word counter stored a count per word per intent. That's a big table full of separate numbers, with nothing shared. Here's a different idea: give each word its own short list of numbers, like a character card in a game. Imagine the card had columns like "how stop-ish", "how all-ish", "how red". Then "halt" and "stop" would have similar cards, and anything the model learns about one would partly carry over to the other.

The twist: we don't decide the columns. Each word starts with 12 random numbers between −0.3 and 0.3, and training nudges them until they're useful. That list is called an embedding. Here are the first 4 of the 12 numbers for "red", from the real model:

"red" before training:  [−0.14, −0.27, −0.20, −0.30, …]   (random)
"red" after training:   [−0.20, −0.76,  0.94, −2.75, …]   (learned)

Do similar words really end up with similar lists? We can measure how much two lists "point the same way" with a score from −1 (opposite) to 1 (identical direction). Real numbers from our model:

pairbefore trainingafter training
halt, stop0.270.92
halt, freeze0.040.92
everything, all−0.560.91
except, but−0.140.57
tea, joke−0.130.10

Nobody told it that "halt" means stop, or that "except" and "but" do the same job. It worked that out because those words led to the same answers in training. ("tea" and "joke" both lead to none, but each appears in only one command, so they barely moved.) That's the big idea behind embeddings, and every LLM starts with exactly this step.

Step 2: read a command by averaging

A command has several words, so several lists. We need one list for the whole thing. The simplest way: average them, position by position. Pretend the lists were only 3 numbers long:

"red"    →  [1, 0, 2]
"block"  →  [3, 2, 0]
average  →  [(1+3)/2, (0+2)/2, (2+0)/2]  =  [2, 1, 1]

Two rules. Words the model has never seen (not in its 88-word vocabulary) are skipped, because there's no list for them. And if no word is known, the result is 12 zeros. That's what read does, and you'll write it.

Predict first: does averaging care about word order?

No. Adding up doesn't care about order, so "put the red block away" and "away block red the put" give exactly the same average. The real model writes ['put', 'red'] for both. For our robot that's mostly fine. For "go to the blue block, not the red one" it's a disaster: "not" can't tell which colour it belongs to. Real LLMs fix part of this with attention, which you can build in the attention stretch.

Step 3: also look at what you've already written

When writing the third word, the model needs to know what it has written so far. So at each step it also looks at the last two words it wrote. Those get their own embeddings (12 numbers each). At the very beginning there's nothing written, so we use a pretend word, <start>. For "put the red and blue blocks away", the four steps look at:

step   the command   2 words back   last word    → should write
1      (average)     <start>        <start>      put
2      (average)     <start>        put          red
3      (average)     put            red          blue
4      (average)     red            blue         <end>

Joined end to end, that's 12 + 12 + 12 = 36 numbers going in at every step. In Python, list + list joins two lists, so this is one line. Notice step 3: "put red" so far, and the command mentions blue, so the next word is blue. Step 4: red and blue are written, so it's time to stop.

You don't need every number in Steps 4–8. They show what happens inside, and they're all real, but you can skim them. For the homework you need four ideas: a neuron adds up inputs times weights (Step 4), softmax with the subtract-the-biggest trick (Step 5), averaging (Step 2) and joining lists (Step 3). The rest is there for when you're curious.

A bridge: from 2 inputs to 36

Week 3's neuron had 2 inputs. Here's one with 3, using made-up numbers so you can check them with a calculator. It works the same way: multiply each input by its weight, add them up, add the bias, then squash. This time the squash is tanh (any number in, a number between −1 and 1 out) instead of sigmoid:

inputs   x = [1,    0,  2   ]
weights  w = [0.5, −1,  0.25]     bias 0.1
sum      = 0.5×1 + (−1)×0 + 0.25×2 + 0.1 = 1.1
output   = tanh(1.1) ≈ 0.80

Now put a second neuron next to it. It reads the same 3 inputs, with its own weights [−0.5, 2, 0] and bias 0: −0.5×1 + 2×0 + 0×2 + 0 = −0.5, and tanh(−0.5) ≈ −0.46. Two neurons side by side are called a layer, and this layer turns 3 numbers into 2: [0.80, −0.46]. Our model's hidden layer is exactly this, only bigger: 36 inputs and 32 neurons side by side.

Step 4: from 36 numbers to 10 scores

Now the full-size version of the bridge. Each of 32 hidden neurons takes all 36 inputs, multiplies each by its own weight, adds them up, adds a bias, and squashes the result with tanh into the range −1 to 1:

hidden[j] = tanh( W1[j][0]×x[0] + W1[j][1]×x[1] + … + W1[j][35]×x[35] + b1[j] )

Then 10 output neurons, one per menu word, each take all 32 hidden values and do the same weighted sum (without the squash). Those 10 numbers are the scores. A tiny check of tanh: math.tanh(0) is 0, math.tanh(2) ≈ 0.96, math.tanh(−2) ≈ −0.96, and math.tanh(100) is 1.0. Big numbers get flattened.

Step 5: from scores to probabilities (softmax)

Scores can be any numbers, even negative. We want probabilities: all positive, adding up to 1. The recipe: raise e (about 2.718) to the power of each score, which makes everything positive and makes bigger scores much bigger, then divide each by the total. In Python that's math.exp. By hand, with three scores:

scores           2        1        0
math.exp(score)  7.389    2.718    1.000      total = 11.107
divide by total  0.665    0.245    0.090      ← adds up to 1

Now a problem. math.exp(1000) is too big for a computer and Python crashes with OverflowError: math range error. The trick: subtract the biggest score from all of them first. Scores 2, 1, 0 become 0, −1, −2:

math.exp(score)  1.000    0.368    0.135      total = 1.503
divide by total  0.665    0.245    0.090      ← exactly the same answer!

Same answer, because shifting every score by the same amount doesn't change their ratios. And now the biggest thing you ever compute is math.exp(0) = 1. No crash. Every real LLM uses this trick.

Step 6: how wrong was it? Loss, or "surprise"

Training needs one number that says how badly the model did. Ours is surprise: if the model gave the right word probability p, the surprise is −math.log(p).

probability it gave the right wordsurprise −log(p)in words
1.000.00"obviously"
0.990.01"I was sure"
0.500.69"coin flip"
0.102.30"no idea" (1 in 10)
0.014.61"I was sure it was something else"

Being confidently wrong is punished hardest. The grown-up name is cross-entropy loss. We add up the surprise for every word of an answer, including the <end>.

Predict first: before any training, roughly what's the surprise per word?

A random model spreads its bets about evenly over 10 words, so each right word gets about 1/10. Surprise ≈ 2.30 per word. An answer has 2 to 5 words counting <end>, so a few units per example. The real program prints first epoch 3.59 (the average per example over the first pass, while it's already learning). By the last pass it's 0.049.

Step 7: where do the examples come from? (synthetic data)

Week 5's word counter learned from 34 example commands. That's nowhere near enough to learn plans: it has no "red and blue" commands and no "everything except" at all. Writing hundreds of examples by hand is slow and boring. So we do what real AI labs do: make the data with a program.

Take a sentence template like "bin {x}". Fill the gap {x} with every colour, every pair of colours, every "all" phrase, and every "except" phrase, and write the right plan next to each. Real output of plan_examples(["bin {x}"], ["everything except the {c}"], random.Random(0)):

('bin the red one', ['put', 'red'])
('bin the blue one', ['put', 'blue'])
('bin the green block', ['put', 'green'])
('bin the red block and the blue block', ['put', 'red', 'blue'])
('bin the red and the green one', ['put', 'red', 'green'])
('bin the blue block and the red block', ['put', 'red', 'blue'])
...                                                   (6 pairs in all)
('bin everything', ['put', 'red', 'blue', 'green'])
...                                                   (4 "all" phrases)
('bin everything except the red', ['put', 'blue', 'green'])
('bin everything except the blue', ['put', 'red', 'green'])
('bin everything except the green', ['put', 'red', 'blue'])

One template gives 3 + 6 + 4 + 3 = 16 examples. Plans always list colours in the same order (red, blue, green), so "the blue block and the red block" is put red blue. That way there's only one right answer to learn. The rng picks between wordings like "the red block" and "the red one", so the examples don't all look identical.

The training set, from training_data():

8 put templates × (3 colours + 6 pairs + 4 "all" + 4 except × 3 colours)  = 200
week 5's 34 commands, repeated 3 times                                    = 102
7 vague commands → ask, repeated 3 times                                  =  21
9 not-a-robot-job commands → none, repeated 3 times                       =  27
                                                                            350
minus every copy of a bake-off command (the robot's exam)                 −   7
                                                                    total = 343

Keep the exam out of the lessons. The 12 bake-off tasks (week 11) are the robot's exam. Three of them would sneak into the training data word for word: "put the red block in the bin" and "stop" are among week 5's 34 examples (3 copies each), and the "bin {x}" template happens to make "bin everything except the green block". So training_data() removes every bake-off command, and 343 examples are left. Why it matters: a model can pass a test question it has already seen just by remembering the answer. Then the score tells you about its memory, not about whether it understands new commands. A test is only honest if the questions are new.

Why repeat the small groups 3 times? Otherwise the 200 template examples drown them out, and the model learns that almost everything is a put.

Held-out test. To check it learned the idea, test_data() builds 50 examples it never trains on, from different templates ("chuck {x} in the trash", "please put {x} in the bin", "all of them except the {c} one"), week 5's 12 TEST commands, and new ask and none commands like "put it in the bin" and "what time is it".

Data matters. Trained on only about 66 hand-written examples, this same model gets just 20–40% of new plans right. The model is the same; the data is the problem. Generating examples from templates fixes it.

Step 8: learning = nudging 2,702 numbers

Where do the good numbers come from? The same trick as week 2's line fitting: gradient descent. For each example, work out which way to nudge each number to make the surprise a bit smaller, then nudge it a little (the learning rate is 0.05). Week 2 nudged 2 numbers. This nudges all of them:

word embeddings      88 words × 12      = 1,056
written-word embs    11 words × 12      =   132   (10 menu words + <start>)
hidden weights       32 × 36            = 1,152
hidden biases        32                 =    32
output weights       10 × 32            =   320
output biases        10                 =    10
                                  total = 2,702 parameters

Working out the nudges uses the chain rule from calculus, so train_step is written for you. Read it if you're curious: the comment softmax + cross-entropy marks the neat line where the "how wrong" for each word is just probability − 1 for the right word and probability − 0 for the others. Training goes through all 343 examples 25 times (25 epochs), in a shuffled order each time. That's 8,575 examples, and it took 10 seconds on our laptop.

Step 9: writing the answer, one word at a time

Once trained, writing is a loop. Read the command once. Then: predict, pick the most likely word, stop if it's <end>, otherwise add it and shift the "last two words" along. Always picking the top word is called greedy decoding. Here are the real probabilities for "put the red and blue blocks away":

step  looks at             top word   its probability
1     <start> <start>      put        1.0000
2     <start> put          red        0.9941   (blue 0.0057)
3     put red              blue       0.9705   (green 0.0148)
4     red blue             <end>      0.9921
→ ['put', 'red', 'blue']  →  [{'intent': 'put', 'object': 'red block'}, {'intent': 'put', 'object': 'blue block'}]

words_to_plan turns the words into a list of actions, and the robot does them one after the other. Before training, the same loop with the random starting numbers writes nothing at all: its very first pick is <end>, with only 0.1181 (green was 0.1123). Same machine, random numbers, no answer. Training is the only difference.

After training it writes exactly the right plan for all 343 training examples (100%) and for 46 of the 50 held-out ones (92%).

Step 10: now break it (this is the important part)

The 4 held-out examples it gets wrong tell you a lot:

'please put everything in the bin'      wanted put red blue green   wrote put red blue
'drive to the block'                    wanted ask                  wrote go red
'where is the block'                    wanted ask                  wrote find red
'wash the dishes'                       wanted none                 wrote find red

It forgets the third colour once, and three times it invents a colour (always red) for a command that has none.

Predict first: what does it write for "where's the green one?"

Just put, then <end> (0.9461, with green at 0.0529). Our tokenizer keeps "where's" as one word, which isn't in the vocabulary, so it's skipped. What's left ("the green one") looks like the start of a put command. But ["put"] with no colour isn't a plan, so words_to_plan returns None and your robot says Sorry, my answer made no sense. A broken answer, caught before the robot moved. That's what words_to_plan's strict rules are for.

Predict first: what does it write for "wash the dishes"?

find red. A complete, well-formed answer, and nonsense. Only "the" is in the vocabulary, and "the" appears in almost every robot command, so the model guesses a robot job and a colour. Compare "what time is it": only "what", "is" and "it" are known. "what" appears in a none example ("what is the weather like"), but "is" appears in find commands ("where is the red block"). Step 1 is a close race, and this time it comes out right: none 0.5250, find 0.3150. Greedy decoding takes the top word even when it barely won, and the final answer hides how unsure it was.

That's a hallucination: a fluent, confident-looking answer that the input doesn't support. Our model now has words for "I don't know" (ask and none), and it uses them for commands that look like its ask and none examples: "make me a sandwich" gives none (0.9950). But it must still pick something. Give it "zzz" (no known words, so it reads 12 zeros) and it writes put red, and it nearly added blue too (<end> 0.5177, blue 0.4672).

Two more, and these matter for a robot:

  • "tidy up the room" gives put red blue. It never saw "room", so it bins two blocks and forgets the green one.
  • "stop the red block" gives go red (go 0.5092, find 0.4839, stop only 0.0034). "stop" is outnumbered by "the red block" in the average. You said stop and the robot drove off. A brain like this must never be trusted on its own. Next week, week 7 builds a safety layer that blocks impossible actions. But watch out: go red is a perfectly allowed action, so the safety layer will not catch this one. Catching it needs a confidence check (try the confidence alarm in the Stretch below).

Is the bake-off fair? Yes: none of its 12 commands is in the training data (Step 7 removes them). So 8/12 is a real score on unseen commands, and so is the 92% on the 50 held-out ones.

4.1 softmax(scores)

Turn any list of scores into probabilities that add up to 1, using Step 5, with the subtract-the-biggest trick.

Example: softmax([2, 1, 0]) → about [0.665, 0.245, 0.090]. softmax([1000, 1000]) → [0.5, 0.5], with no crash.

Hint 1

Three stages: find the biggest score, make a list of math.exp(score − biggest), divide each by the total.

Hint 2

max(scores) and sum(...) do the first and last stages. A list comprehension does the middle one.

Hint 3

m = max(scores); exps = [math.exp(s - m) for s in scores]; then return each e / total where total = sum(exps).

4.2 TinyLM.read(self, command)

The average embedding of the command's known words (Step 2). Tokenize the command, keep only words in self.word_emb, and average their lists position by position. If no word is known, return [0.0] * E.

Example: for a model with vocabulary {"red", "block"}, read("red block") is the average of the "red" and "block" lists, and read("zzz qqq") is twelve 0.0s.

Hint 1

First collect a list of the embeddings you'll average. Unknown words must be skipped, not crash with a KeyError.

Hint 2

Handle "nothing known" before dividing, or you'll divide by zero. For the average, position i of the result is the sum of position i of every list, divided by how many lists.

Hint 3

vecs = [self.word_emb[w] for w in tokenize(command) if w in self.word_emb]. If vecs is empty return zeros; otherwise build a list where item i is sum(v[i] for v in vecs) / len(vecs), for i in range(E).

4.3 TinyLM.forward(self, ctx, prev2, prev1)

One prediction step (Steps 3–5). Join ctx with the written-word embeddings of prev2 and prev1 (from self.out_emb) into 36 numbers, x. Compute the 32 hidden values, then the 10 scores, then softmax. Return (x, hidden, probs): train_step needs all three.

Example: forward(model.read("red block"), START, START) returns an x of length 36, a hidden of length 32, and 10 probabilities adding to 1.

New Python this time: zip, and sum of a loop. zip walks two lists side by side, and sum(...) can add up a whole loop in one line. Together they do the bridge's "inputs times weights, added up": 
w, x = [0.5, -1, 0.25], [1, 0, 2]
print(sum(a * b for a, b in zip(w, x)))      # 0.5*1 + -1*0 + 0.25*2 = 1.0
Hint 1

self.W1 is a list of 32 rows, each with 36 weights, and self.b1 has 32 biases. self.W2 is 10 rows of 32, and self.b2 has 10. Start with x = ctx + self.out_emb[prev2] + self.out_emb[prev1] (three 12-number lists joined into 36).

Hint 2

Make it easy on yourself: write a tiny helper for one neuron first, just like the bridge. You can put it inside forward:

def neuron(row, inputs, bias):
    return sum(w * v for w, v in zip(row, inputs)) + bias

A hidden value is math.tanh(neuron(row, x, b)), once for each row, b in zip(self.W1, self.b1). A score is neuron(row, hidden, b) (no tanh), once for each row, b in zip(self.W2, self.b2).

Hint 3

Fill in the blanks (under your neuron helper):

x = ctx + self.out_emb[prev2] + self.out_emb[____]
hidden = [math.tanh(neuron(row, ____, b)) for row, b in zip(self.W1, self.b1)]
scores = [neuron(row, ____, b) for row, b in zip(self.W2, self.b2)]
return x, hidden, ____(scores)

4.4 TinyLM.generate(self, command, max_words=MAX_WORDS)

Greedy decoding (Step 9). Read the command once. Start with prev2 = prev1 = START. Up to max_words times (5 by default): forward, take the word in OUT_WORDS with the biggest probability, stop if it's "<end>", otherwise append it and shift. Return the words, without "<end>".

Example (after training): generate("put the red and blue blocks away") → ['put', 'red', 'blue'].

Hint 1

Call self.read once, before the loop. The command doesn't change while you write.

Hint 2

The position of the biggest probability is probs.index(max(probs)), and OUT_WORDS at that position is the word.

Hint 3

Loop for _ in range(max_words): get probs from self.forward(ctx, prev2, prev1) (you only need the third thing it returns), pick the word, break on "<end>", else append it and do prev2, prev1 = prev1, word.

4.5 words_to_plan(words)

Turn the model's words into something the robot can do: a list of action dictionaries (the same shape as week 5's parse_command), or the word "ask" or "none". Return None if the words don't make sense.

["ask"]                 → "ask"
["none"]                → "none"
["stop"]                → [{"intent": "stop", "object": None}]
["go", "red"]           → [{"intent": "go", "object": "red block"}]      (go and find: exactly one colour)
["put", "red", "blue"]  → [{"intent": "put", "object": "red block"}, {"intent": "put", "object": "blue block"}]
["go", "red", "blue"]   → None   (go can't go to two places)
["put", "red", "red"]   → None   (a repeated colour)
["red", "put"], ["put", "purple"], ["put"], [], ["stop", "red"]  → None
Hint 1

Deal with the three one-word answers first (["ask"], ["none"], ["stop"]). Python can compare whole lists: words == ["stop"].

Hint 2

Everything else must start with put, go or find, followed by at least one colour. colours = words[1:]. Every colour must be in COLOURS, and len(set(colours)) != len(colours) means there's a repeat. Only put may have more than one colour.

Hint 3

After the one-word checks: if not words or words[0] isn't in ("put", "go", "find"), return None. Check the colours as in Hint 2, and that a go/find has exactly one. Then return [{"intent": words[0], "object": f"{c} block"} for c in colours].

4.6 plan_examples(put_templates, except_templates, rng)

Make the synthetic data from Step 7. For every template in put_templates, add, in this order:

  1. each colour c: the template with x = rng.choice(["the c block", "the c one"]) → ["put", c];
  2. each ordered pair (a, b) of different colours (6 pairs): x = rng.choice(["the a and b blocks", "the a block and the b block", "the a and the b one"]) → ["put", a, b] with the colours in COLOURS order;
  3. each phrase in ALL_PHRASES: x = the phrase → ["put", "red", "blue", "green"];
  4. each except template e and each colour c: x = e.format(c=c) → ["put"] + the other two colours, in COLOURS order.

Return a list of (command, plan words) pairs. Loop in exactly this order (colours in COLOURS order) so your rng choices match the checker's.

Example: plan_examples(["bin {x}"], ["everything except the {c}"], random.Random(0)) gives the 16 examples printed in Step 7, starting with ('bin the red one', ['put', 'red']) and ending with ('bin everything except the green', ['put', 'red', 'blue']).

Hint 1

Fill a template with t.format(x=...). One outer loop over put_templates, with four loops inside it, one per numbered step.

Hint 2

For the pairs, loop a over COLOURS, then b over COLOURS, and skip a == b. To write the colours in COLOURS order, don't use [a, b]: use [k for k in COLOURS if k in (a, b)]. The except plan is [k for k in COLOURS if k != c].

Hint 3

Call rng.choice exactly once per colour in step 1 and once per pair in step 2, and nowhere else. An extra or missing call shifts every later choice, and the checker's "first: the red block" test will fail. Use f-strings for the choices, e.g. [f"the {c} block", f"the {c} one"].

5. Real-world challenge: be the language model

You'll play the model, with a friend or family member, and measure your own loss and your own hallucinations. You need 10 coins (or beans, or Lego bricks), paper and a pencil.

  1. Round 1: next word, with bets. Your partner secretly writes a short, ordinary sentence of 6–8 words (e.g. something they'd text you). They tell you the first word. Before each next word, you split your 10 coins between up to three guesses. Then they reveal the real word.
  2. Score your surprise for each word from the coins you put on the right word, using this table (it's −log(coins/10) from Step 6): 10 coins → 0, 8 → 0.22, 5 → 0.69, 3 → 1.20, 2 → 1.61, 1 → 2.30, 0 → 4.6 (a penalty, as if you'd bet 1 coin in 100). Add up the sentence. Do 3 sentences, then swap roles.
  3. Notice how you bet. After "put the kettle", were you nearly sure of "on"? After "I", were your coins spread out? That's softmax: a sharp or a flat spread of probability.
  4. Round 2: no "I don't know" allowed. Your partner asks 5 questions you can't possibly know the answer to, like "What's the name of my teacher's dog?" or "What did I eat on 3 March last year?" Rule: you must answer instantly, with one confident specific answer. Write down each answer and whether it was right.
  5. Round 3: same game, but "I don't know" is allowed. Ask 5 new unknowable questions. Count how often you use it.

On your own (no partner needed): for Round 1, open a book you haven't read at a random page and cover everything below the first line with a card. Read the first word of a sentence, bet your coins on the next word, then slide the card along to check. For Rounds 2 and 3, open an atlas or a fact book at a random page and cover the facts. Ask yourself 5 things you can't know (the height of a mountain, the year a town was founded): in Round 2 answer instantly with a specific guess, in Round 3 you may say "I don't know". Then check.

Record: your total surprise for each sentence (who was the better "model"?), your 5 forced answers from Round 2 and how many were right, and how Round 3 felt different. What it should teach you: a good language model isn't one that's always sure; it's one whose surprise is low on real text. And when you take away the "I don't know" option, you get confident nonsense, every time. That's not a bug in you, or in TinyLM. It's what happens to any system that must always answer.

7. Common mistakes

  • Not subtracting the biggest score in softmax. Fine for small scores, then OverflowError on big ones. Checker hint: "subtract the biggest score first".
  • Averaging over all tokens instead of known ones, or looking up unknown words (KeyError), or dividing by zero when none are known. Checker hint: "no known words -> a list of E zeros".
  • Making x the wrong length. ctx + emb + emb joins three 12-number lists into 36. If you add them number by number you get 12, and the weights won't line up. Checker hint: "x has 3*E numbers, hidden has H".
  • Forgetting to shift prev2, prev1 in generate. Then every step sees the same input and writes the same word again and again.
  • Accepting nonsense in words_to_plan. ["put", "red", "red"] and ["go", "red", "blue"] must be None. Checker hints: "a repeated colour makes no sense -> None" and "go/find take exactly one colour".
  • Colours in the wrong order in plan_examples. "the blue block and the red block" is ["put", "red", "blue"]. Checker hint: "the pair (blue, red) must be written in COLOURS order -> ['put', 'red', 'blue'], not [a, b] (sort the pair by COLOURS)".
  • Calling rng.choice in the wrong places. One call per colour and one per pair, nothing for the "all" and except examples. Otherwise the wordings don't match. Checker hint: "first: the red block, plan ['put', 'red']".

8. Check yourself

What is an embedding, and who chooses its numbers?

A short list of numbers standing for a word (12 here). Nobody chooses them: they start random and training nudges them. Words used the same way end up with similar lists, like "halt" and "stop", or "everything" and "all".

Predict: what does softmax([5, 5, 5, 5]) return?

[0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25]. Equal scores, equal chances. Only the differences between scores matter, which is also why subtracting the biggest is safe.

Predict: what does model.generate("halt halt halt") return, after training?

['stop'], exactly the same as generate("halt"). The average of three identical lists is that same list, so the model reads exactly the same 12 numbers.

Why do we make training examples from templates instead of using week 5's 34 commands?

34 commands have no "red and blue" or "everything except" examples at all, so there's nothing to learn plans from. Templates give 200 plan examples in seconds. The catch: the model only learns the wordings the templates contain, which is why "tidy up the room" still fails.

Why test on held-out examples instead of the training examples?

The model gets 100% of its training examples right, which only shows it memorised them. On 50 examples written with different templates it gets 92%. That's the number that tells you how it will do on commands people actually type.

If the model gives the right word probability 0.5, what's the surprise? And 0.99?

−log(0.5) ≈ 0.69 and −log(0.99) ≈ 0.01. Training pushes the right words towards probability 1, so surprise towards 0.

The model has a "stop" word. Why does "stop the red block" get go red?

The average mixes "stop" with "the", "red" and "block", which together look like a go or find command. Step 1 is a near tie between go (0.5092) and find (0.4839), and stop gets only 0.0034. Greedy decoding writes go, and the final answer hides how close it was. Week 7's safety layer won't catch it either, because go red is an allowed action. Only a confidence check (or a person) would notice.
