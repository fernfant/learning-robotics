Start here

A language model does one thing, over and over: read everything so far, give every possible next word a chance, pick one, repeat.

Last week's brain sorts a command into one pile, so "put the red and blue blocks away" bins only the red block. This week's brain writes its answer, as long as the job needs. Think of a friend finishing your sentence: "put the red block in the…" "bin!" They've heard enough sentences that the next word just feels likely.

Embedding
A short list of numbers (12 here) that stands for a word. The model learns them.
Softmax
Turns any list of scores into chances that add up to 1.
Loss (surprise)
How surprised the model was by the right answer. Training makes it small.
Parameter
One learnable number. Ours has 2,702. Big LLMs have billions.

1. The idea

💡 The idea: write one word, then the next

The model writes from a menu of 10 words: put go find stop ask none red blue green <end>. At every step it looks at your command plus the last two words it wrote, turns that into numbers, gives each menu word a chance, and writes the top one. Real numbers for "put the red and blue blocks away":

Each written word goes back in as "the last word" for the next step. <end> means "I've finished". With the random numbers it starts with, the same machine writes nonsense. Training is the only difference.

💡 The idea: words as dots on a map

Every word gets 12 numbers, like a position on a map. They start random. Training nudges them, and words that lead to the same answers drift together. Here are 10 real trained words, with their 12 numbers squashed flat to 2 so we can draw them:

Nobody told it that "halt" means stop. It worked that out because they led to the same plans. How alike are two words' 12 numbers? On a scale from −1 to 1: halt and stop went from 0.27 before training to 0.92 after; everything and all from −0.56 to 0.91. Every LLM starts with this step.

💡 The idea: scores into chances (softmax)

The network gives each menu word a raw score. Softmax raises e (about 2.718) to each score, so all are positive and big scores get much bigger, then divides by the total:

scores           2        1        0
math.exp(score)  7.389    2.718    1.000      total 11.107
chance           0.665    0.245    0.090      ← adds up to 1

One trap: math.exp(1000) crashes. So subtract the biggest score first (2, 1, 0 becomes 0, −1, −2). You get exactly the same chances, and nothing is ever bigger than math.exp(0) = 1.

5. Real-world challenge: be the language model

🌍 Real-world challenge
  1. A partner secretly writes a 6–8 word sentence and tells you the first word. Before each next word, split 10 coins between up to three guesses. Then they reveal it.
  2. Score your surprise from the coins on the right word: 10 → 0, 8 → 0.22, 5 → 0.69, 3 → 1.20, 2 → 1.61, 1 → 2.30, 0 → 4.6. Add up each sentence. Swap roles.
  3. Then they ask 5 questions you can't know ("What's my teacher's dog called?"). You must answer instantly and confidently. Then 5 more where "I don't know" is allowed.

On your own: cover a page of an unread book with a card, bet your coins on each next word, and slide the card to check. The lesson: a good model has low surprise on real text, and taking away "I don't know" makes confident nonsense. That's hallucination.

6. Check yourself

What is an embedding, and who chooses its numbers?

A short list of numbers for a word. Nobody chooses them: they start random and training nudges them. Words used the same way end up close.

Predict: softmax([5, 5, 5, 5])?

[0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25]. Only differences between scores matter.

Predict: generate("halt halt halt") after training?

['stop'], the same as "halt". The average of identical lists is that list.

Why test on held-out examples?

It gets 100% of its training examples, which only shows memory. On 50 new ones it gets 92%: that's the honest number.

Why does "stop the red block" give go red?

The average mixes "stop" with "the red block", which looks like go or find. Step 1 is a near tie (go 0.5092, find 0.4839) and greedy writing hides it.

7. Want to know more?

Inside one step: averaging, 36 inputs and tanh

Reading by averaging. With 3-number lists: "red" [1, 0, 2] and "block" [3, 2, 0] average to [2, 1, 1]. Averaging ignores order, so "away block red the put" gives the same plan as "put the red block away". For "go to the blue block, not the red one" that's a disaster. Attention fixes part of this: see the stretch.

A neuron with 3 inputs (made-up numbers): inputs [1, 0, 2], weights [0.5, −1, 0.25], bias 0.1. Sum 0.5 + 0 + 0.5 + 0.1 = 1.1, and tanh(1.1) ≈ 0.80. tanh squashes any number into −1 to 1: math.tanh(2) ≈ 0.96, math.tanh(100) is 1.0. Our hidden layer is 32 of these side by side, each reading all 36 inputs (the command's 12 + two written words' 12 each).
