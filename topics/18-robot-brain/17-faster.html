Don't think twice about the same question (caching). Store numbers with fewer marks (quantising). Let a big brain teach a small one (distilling). Each trick saves a lot, and each has one way it quietly goes wrong.

Robo-Helper hears "stop" forty times a day and works it out from scratch every time. Its brain stores every number with 32 bits when 4 might do. And it still gets "where's the green one?" wrong. Three kinds of waste, three fixes.

Cache
A store of answers you've already worked out. Say it "cash".
Hit / miss
Hit: the answer was stored. Miss: it wasn't, so you had to think.
Quantise
Round numbers to a few allowed values, so each needs fewer bits.
Distil
Train a small "student" on answers written by a big "teacher".

1. The idea

💡 Caching: a notebook of answers

Keep a notebook. Before thinking, look the question up. If it's there, read it out: instant and free. If not, think, then write it down. The catch: "where is the red block?" has a different answer once someone moves the block. So the notebook files every answer under the words and the world.

Tidying the words with week 7's tokenize means "Put the RED block!" and "put the red block" share a key: "put the red block|w1".

💡 Quantising: a ruler with fewer marks

Imagine a ruler with only a few marks. You round every measurement to the nearest mark, and only write down which mark it was. Fewer marks means fewer bits, but bigger jumps. 8 bits: 255 marks. 4 bits: 15 marks. 2 bits: just 3.

Real result on week 8's attention model (2,702 numbers). Blue: how big the model is. Green: how many held-out plans it still gets right.

8 and 4 bits are free wins: 8× smaller, no loss. At 3 bits the close calls flip first (colours go wrong). At 2 bits it writes nonsense.

💡 Distilling: teacher and student

Labelling examples by hand is slow. So a big, clever teacher labels new commands, and our small, fast student trains on those labels. You get some of the teacher's smarts at the student's speed. The catch: a student copies its teacher's mistakes.

Real result: three students. Each is tested on the usual held-out plans (an easy test) and on 6 harder commands ("hard-6"):

The real numbers

The word counter labelled "where's …" commands as put, and its student believed it. The careful human's student gets 10/12. Notice the easy held-out test ranked the worst teacher first: only the harder tests showed the truth.

6. Check yourself

You ask "Halt!", "halt", "HALT" (world "w"), then "halt" (world "w2"). Hits, misses, hit rate?

The first three share the key "halt|w": 1 miss, 2 hits. "halt|w2" is new: another miss. 2 hits, 2 misses, 50%.

What does quantize([0.9, -0.4, 0.2], 3) give, and what comes back?

top = 3, scale = 0.9 ÷ 3 = 0.3. Python says ([3, -1, 1], 0.3). Back: [0.9, -0.3, 0.3]. The biggest survives exactly; the others are 0.1 off.

The word counter's student did worse on hard-6 (83% → 67%). How do you protect against a bad teacher?

Use a better teacher, check a sample of its labels by hand, and always test on a hard set. The easy held-out test even ranked the bad teacher first.

Want to know more?

Why each row gets its own scale, and a floating-point surprise

If one row has numbers around 1.5 and another around 0.01, one shared scale would round the whole small row to zero. So quantized_copy gives every row its own.

Try quantize([0.6, -0.3, 0.1], 3). By hand 0.1 ÷ 0.2 = 0.5, which rounds to even, 0. But Python says ([3, -2, 1], 0.19999999999999998): a computer can't store 0.2 exactly. One more reason to test, not trust the maths.

How real companies use all three

Prompt caching: the server remembers its work on the unchanging start of your prompt (rules, tool list), so put those first and the changing parts last. Quantising: big models are often served at 8 or 4 bits, so they fit on fewer machines and write faster. Distilling: a big model writes examples and a small one handles everyday requests. Fine-tuning a big student cheaply is Part B: LoRA. With a grown-up and an API key, python3 brains.py record lets Claude label the 24 commands, and best_teacher() then prefers its labels.
