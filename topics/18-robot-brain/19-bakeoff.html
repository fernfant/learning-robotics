Start here

To compare brains fairly, give every one the exact same tasks. Then look at the room afterwards. Don't trust what the brain says it did.

The problem

Your friend says their brain is "way smarter" than yours. They show you one command where it worked. You show them one where yours worked. Nobody learns anything. And here's a sneaky one: ask the word counter "where's the green one?" and it replies, politely, "The green block is in the bin." That sounds like an answer. But it's true because the robot just drove over and binned it. Nobody asked for that.

The idea in one picture

Think of a baking contest on TV. Every baker gets the same recipe, the same ingredients, the same oven and the same time. The judges don't listen to the bakers explain how lovely their cake is: they cut it open and taste it. And they don't only score taste. A brilliant cake that took nine hours and cost £200 in ingredients might not be the one you'd want to make every Saturday.

Words to know

Benchmark
A fixed set of tasks used to compare different systems fairly. Ours is the 12 tasks in TASKS.
Behaviour-level check
Judging a task by what really happened: what's in the bin, where the robot is, and if it asked a question. Not by the words it said.
Scorecard
One summary per brain: how many tasks it passed, how long it thought, and what it cost.
Failure category
A group of failures with the same cause, such as "can only do one block at a time".
Trade-off
When getting more of one good thing means giving up some of another. Like being right versus being fast.
Replay
A recording of everything the robot did, frame by frame, that you can watch again later.

The full lesson: everything below builds a fair test from scratch, reads the real results, then turns them into a design decision. You write the judge and the scorecard.

More words you'll meet below

Hybrid system
A design that uses different brains for different jobs, such as a fast local one for reflexes and a big one for hard thinking.
Scorecard numbers

1. The problem

In Lesson 11 you tested one brain on 12 commands and checked whether result["ok"] was true and which blocks were binned. Now there are several brains, built in completely different ways. One replies with one action, another writes a whole plan, another calls tools ten times. Some ask questions. They don't even produce the same kind of output.

So what can you compare? Only one thing is the same for all of them: the robot and the room. Every brain drives the same SimRobot, starting at (1, 1), with the cat hiding at (4, 1). So the fair question is: after the brain has finished, is the room in the state the person wanted?

And then a second question that real products always face: if two brains both get it right, but one takes a fraction of a millisecond and runs on your laptop, while the other needs the internet and costs money for every command, which do you ship?

2. First principles

Step 1: judge the room, not the reply

The obvious way to test a brain is to read its reply. Let's try it on the real result for task 2, "where's the green one?", with the word counter:

reply: "The green block is in the bin."

Is that a good answer? You can't tell from the words. It could be a correct "find" answer (if the block was already in the bin) or the result of doing the wrong thing. So look at the world instead. Before: nothing in the bin, robot at (1, 1). After: the green block is in the bin, and the robot is parked at (7, 6). The person asked a question and the robot tidied something up. Fail.

That's a behaviour-level check: compare the room after the run with what a correct robot would leave behind. It works for every kind of brain, because every brain ends up moving the same robot.

Step 2: write down "correct" before you run anything

Each task in TASKS says what a correct robot ends up doing. There are five kinds of check:

Step 4: predict, then look

Predict first: task 12 is "put the purple block in the bin". There is no purple block. What does the tiny LM do? What does the word counter do?

Tiny LM: it asks "Which block do you mean: red, blue or green?" Pass. Here's why. "purple" isn't a word it has ever seen, so it ignores it. What's left reads like "put the block in the bin", which is one of the training examples where the right answer is ask. It passed by asking, which is the safe thing to do, though it doesn't know purple blocks don't exist.

Word counter: it passes too. It finds no known colour word, so the object is None, and validate_action refuses: "'put' needs an object". The robot stays still. It passed by refusing, not by understanding: it would do the same for "put the thing in the bin".

Each brain you built made the robot better, and you can see exactly where. A score alone tells you little. Group the failures by cause (failure categories), and the picture gets sharp:

  • One action only (tasks 4, 6, and 8's question). The word counter outputs exactly one {"intent", "object"}, so "two blocks", "everything except" and "which one?" can't even be expressed. Lesson 15 fixed this by changing the output format: the tiny LM writes a whole plan, or ask.
  • Hears the wrong intent (tasks 2, 11). "where's" and "what about" weren't in the templates the tiny LM and the attention model learned from, so they guess "put" (or, for the tiny LM on task 2, write a plan that makes no sense). Lesson 17's distillation fixed this: the teacher's labels included "where's the red one" and friends, and the distilled brain now answers instead of binning.
  • An unseen word (task 5). "room" is a word none of the small brains has ever seen. They ignore it, and what's left, "tidy up the", is vague. The attention model asks which block. The others guess: the tiny LM bins red and blue, and the distilled and 4-bit brains bin only green.
  • No idea where things are (task 7). "Closest" needs the room. No language-only model ever sees robot.look(), so no amount of training on words can fix it. That's what week 10's Claude, with the whole room in its prompt, is for.

Notice: more (and better) training examples fixed "hears the wrong intent". They could never have fixed "one action only": that needed a new output format. And task 7 needs a brain that can see the room. Knowing which kind of failure you have tells you what to do next. Squeezing the distilled brain to 4 bits didn't cost a single task.

Remember Lesson 16's thinking distance: at 1 metre per second, a robot that keeps moving while its brain thinks travels 1 metre for every second of thinking. Our local brains think for a few thousandths of a second at most. A world-class model has to send your request across the internet and run a huge network on it, so expect its column to be measured in a very different unit. Fill in the real numbers from your own run, or from the recordings.

Predict first: a brain passes 12/12 but its p95 thinking time is several seconds. Would you let it handle "stop"?

No. "Stop" is exactly the command where waiting is dangerous. If the robot keeps rolling while the request travels to a data centre and back, the correct answer arrives too late. Correctness and speed are separate scores, and for some tasks speed is part of being correct.

Step 7: the real-world answer is usually "both"

So which brain wins? Look at what each one is good at. The local brains are instant, free, and work with the Wi-Fi off, but they understand very little. A world-class model understands nearly everything but is slow, costs money per command, and disappears when the internet does. Real robots, cars and phones don't choose one. They use a hybrid:

always, on the robot (reflexes, never wait for the internet):
    stop-neuron + A* replanning around surprises     (Lessons 4, 6, 11)
    "stop" / "halt" / "freeze"  → stop NOW            (word counter, ~0.01 ms in Lesson 16)

a command arrives:
    simple and the local brain is sure?   → do it locally
    otherwise                             → ask the world-class planner
                                            → validate_plan (Lesson 18)  → act
    no internet, or no answer in time?    → "Sorry, I didn't catch that." (don't guess)

Your body works the same way. Touch a hot pan and your hand jerks back before your brain has even noticed: a fast, simple reflex. Deciding what to cook for dinner takes the slow, clever part. And Lesson 17 showed one more trick: distil a teacher's answers into a small local brain, so more commands can be handled fast and free over time. With just 24 careful labels it took the small brain from 8/12 to 10/12.

4.1 score_task(task, robot, result)

Did the robot do the right thing? Return (passed, reason). Check in this order, and return at the first failure:

  1. robot.binned == task["binned"], else (False, "binned X, wanted Y").
  2. If task.get("still"): the robot must be at ROBOT_START, else (False, "moved but shouldn't have").
  3. If "at" is in the task: the robot must be at task["at"], else (False, "ended at P, wanted Q").
  4. If "says" is in the task: that text must be inside result["reply"].
  5. If task.get("asks"): the reply, stripped of spaces, must end with "?".

All passed → (True, "ok"). Examples from the real run: word counter on task 5 → (False, "binned nothing, wanted ['blue block', 'green block', 'red block']"); on task 8 → (False, "should ask a question").

Hint 1

Five ifs, each with its own return False, ..., then return True, "ok" at the end. The order matters: it decides which reason you report when several things are wrong.

Hint 2

robot.pos might be a list or a tuple, so compare tuple(robot.pos) with ROBOT_START. To print the sets nicely and the same way every time, use sorted(robot.binned) or 'nothing': an empty list is falsy, so or swaps in the word. Some replies may be missing: result.get("reply", "").

Hint 3
reply = result.get("reply", "")
binned differs                       → False, f"binned {sorted(...) or 'nothing'}, wanted {sorted(...) or 'nothing'}"
task.get("still") and pos != start   → False, "moved but shouldn't have"
"at" in task and pos != task["at"]   → False, f"ended at {robot.pos}, wanted {task['at']}"
"says" in task and not in reply      → False, f"reply missing {task['says']!r}"
task.get("asks") and not reply.strip().endswith("?") → False, "should ask a question"
→ True, "ok"

4.2 scorecard(rows)

rows is one brain's list of {"passed", "seconds", "tokens_in", "tokens_out"}. Return {"passed", "total", "p50", "p95", "cost"}. Example: the check.py rows from Step 6 → {"passed": 2, "total": 3, "p50": 2.0, "p95": 3.0, "cost": 0.012}.

Hint 1

You already wrote the hard parts in Lesson 16. They're imported at the top: summarize and cost_usd.

Hint 2

summarize wants a list of seconds: build it with a list comprehension. True counts as 1 in Python, so sum(r["passed"] for r in rows) counts the passes. Add up all the input tokens and all the output tokens first, then call cost_usd once.

Hint 3
s = summarize([seconds of each row])
return {"passed": number of rows that passed, "total": len(rows),
        "p50": s["p50"], "p95": s["p95"],

Stretch 2: an AI model as the judge

Our bake-off checks are exact: we look in the bin, at where the robot ended up, at whether it asked a question. That only works because our world is tiny. Now imagine grading an essay, a chat reply or a summary. There's no bin to look in. So companies often ask another AI model to grade the answer. That's called LLM-as-a-judge. Judges are useful, and also dangerous. This stretch shows you both.

Rule 1: measure the judge against checks you trust

A judge is just another model that can be wrong. Before you trust it, run it on tasks where you already know the right verdict, and count how often it agrees. Here we're lucky: our exact checks are the trusted answers. The judge's agreement is the fraction of tasks where its pass/fail matches the exact check. The tasks where they disagree are the interesting ones.

Start with the cheapest judge possible, rule_judge (provided). It reads only the reply and says "pass" unless the reply sounds like a refusal ("sorry", "can't", …). A confident report and a polite question both sound perfectly reasonable, so the judge caught neither.

Rule 2: compare with a lazy judge (kappa)

Agreement on its own can fool you. Imagine a robot that passes 9 tasks out of 10. A lazy judge that says "pass" to everything agrees 90% of the time, and it has never caught a single mistake. So always ask two more questions:

  • Catch rate: of the runs that really failed, how many did the judge fail? The rule judge caught 3 of 7 on the word counter, 1 of 4 on the tiny LM and 0 of 2 on the distilled brain.
  • Kappa (Cohen's kappa): how much better the judge agrees than a guesser with the same pass rate would by luck. 0 means no better than guessing, 1 means perfect. Below 0 means worse than guessing. The usual bar before you let a judge make decisions is about 0.6. Our rule judge scores 0.21, 0.14 and −0.12.

The distilled brain shows the trap at its worst. The rule judge agrees with the exact checks on 75% of its tasks, which sounds good. But a lazy judge that says "pass" to everything agrees on 83%, because the brain passes 10 of 12. The better your robot gets, the easier it is for a useless judge to look good, and only kappa gives it away.

Module 19's article found exactly this with a real AI judge on 60 support replies: 60% agreement sounded fine, but "always pass" would have scored 57%, and kappa was only 0.15 (Module 19).

Give the judge the evidence

The fix is the one from Step 1: show the judge what actually happened in the room, not just what the robot said. Your evidence_text turns a run's recorded frames into one sentence, and judge_prompt adds it to the prompt. Here's the real example the file prints:

A person told a cleaning robot: "where's the green one?"
The robot replied: 'The green block is in the bin.'
What actually happened: Robot started at (1, 1) and ended at (7, 6). In the bin at the end: green block. It moved: yes.
Did the robot do the right thing? A correct robot does exactly what was asked, refuses impossible or unrelated requests without moving, and asks a question when the request is ambiguous.
Predict first: you're the judge. Pass or fail, with and without the "What actually happened" line?

Without it, "The green block is in the bin." is a perfectly good answer to "where's the green one?". With it, you can see the robot drove off and binned the block when it was only asked a question: fail. Same reply, different evidence, different verdict.

Asking Claude to judge

ask_judge (provided) sends the prompt to Claude with a short system prompt ("a strict, fair judge") and asks for structured output: JSON that must match a schema with exactly two fields, verdict ("pass" or "fail") and reason. Forcing a schema means your code can read the verdict without guessing what a sentence meant. With an API key, python3 lesson19_judge.py record asks Claude to judge every run twice, once reply-only and once with the evidence, and saves the verdicts to recordings/judge.json. After that, the plain python3 lesson19_judge.py adds two more agreement lines per brain.

Whatever the numbers turn out to be, the question to ask is the same: how much does the evidence line change the agreement, and on which tasks does even the evidence-reading judge still disagree with the exact checks?

Rule 3: every score gets an error bar

The word counter scored 5/12, the tiny LM 8/12 and the distilled brain 10/12. Is each one really better than the last? Twelve tasks is very few. If we'd picked a slightly different 12, the scores could easily have come out differently. A bootstrap tells you how much: pretend your 12 results are a bag, draw 12 out at random (putting each one back), score that pretend test, and do it 2,000 times. The middle 95% of those pretend scores is your error bar. Real output:

Honest numbers: each score with a 95% error bar (bootstrap over the 12 tasks)
  words (Lesson 9)              5/12   somewhere between  17% and  67%
  tinylm (Lesson 15)            8/12   somewhere between  42% and  92%
  distilled (Lesson 17)        10/12   somewhere between  58% and 100%
  paired: tinylm minus words = +25% [+0%, +50%]  (they differ on 3 task(s); too close to call)
  paired: distilled minus words = +42% [+17%, +67%]  (they differ on 5 task(s); a real difference)

The error bars are huge, and they overlap a lot. The paired lines compare two brains task by task, which is fairer, because both sat the exact same tasks. The tiny LM beat the word counter on 3 tasks, but its interval touches zero (+0%), so with only 12 tasks we can't be sure it's a real win: "too close to call". The distilled brain differs from the word counter on 5 tasks, all in its favour, and its whole interval is above zero (+17% to +67%): a real difference, even with 12 tasks. To tell close brains apart reliably, you need many more tasks. That's why real evals use hundreds.

Judge pitfalls

  • Agreeing with confident text. As above. Give the judge evidence wherever you can.
  • Position bias. When a judge compares two answers, it often prefers whichever comes first (or second). Ask twice with the order swapped.
  • Length bias. Judges often prefer longer, more detailed answers, even when the short one is right.
  • A judge is a model too. It can misunderstand the task, hallucinate a reason, or change its mind on a re-run. That's why you measure it against trusted checks first, and keep checking.

5. Real-world challenge: the family tidy-up bake-off

Run a real bake-off with people as the brains. You need 3 small objects (say a sock, a pen and a toy), a box as the bin, index cards, a stopwatch (a phone is fine) and two or three "contestants".

  1. Write 8 task cards, like TASKS: a simple one ("put the sock in the box"), two objects, "everything except the pen", "the one closest to the box", an ambiguous one with two similar objects ("put the pen away" when there are two pens), an impossible one ("put the sofa in the box"), a question ("where's the toy?") and "stop".
  2. On the back of each card, write the pass rule first: exactly what's in the box afterwards, whether they should move at all, and whether they should ask a question. Do this before anyone plays.
  3. Contestants. One normal human. One "word-counter human", who must follow a strict rule: act on the first object word they hear, and never ask questions. Optionally a third, who must write down a full plan before moving (a planner).
  4. Run every card on every contestant, resetting the room each time (fresh robot!). Time from the end of the instruction to their first movement: that's thinking time.
  5. Judge the room, not the words. Use only the rule on the back of the card. Record ✓/✗, the reason, and the time, in a table like the one in Step 5.
  6. Work out the scorecard: passes out of 8, and the median thinking time (sort the times, take the middle).

What it teaches: the strict word-counter human is the fastest and fails exactly the tasks our word counter failed. Normal humans are slower but ask "which pen?". Writing the rules first stops you being generous to the contestant you like. And you'll notice how often you want to judge by what someone says instead of what they did.

7. Common mistakes

  • Checking "the wanted blocks are in the bin" instead of "exactly these blocks". Then a robot that bins everything passes "put the red block in the bin". check.py: "an EXTRA block binned -> fail".
  • Comparing a list with a tuple. [1, 1] == (1, 1) is False in Python. Use tuple(robot.pos). Otherwise a robot that stayed still "moved". check.py: "stayed still -> pass".
  • Forgetting to strip. "Which block? " ends with a space, not "?". check.py: "a question (ending in '?') -> pass".
  • Pricing each row and adding the costs is fine, but pricing only the first row, or mixing up input and output prices, isn't. check.py: "tokens 3,000 in and 0 out cost $0.012".
  • Judging by the reply. "The green block is in the bin." is a lovely sentence and a failed task. Look at the room.
  • Trusting a judge you haven't measured (Stretch 2). On the distilled brain, a reply-only judge agreed with the exact checks 75% of the time, less than the 83% a lazy always-pass judge gets (kappa −0.12). Measure any judge with agreement first, and give it the evidence. check.py: "with evidence -> a line 'What actually happened: ...'".

8. Check yourself

Why does every brain get a fresh robot for every task?

So every run starts from the same room. If task 1 binned the red block, task 4 would start with a different room for one brain than another, and the comparison would no longer be fair.

Predict: a brain answers "stop" to every single command. What does it score?

It never moves and bins nothing, so it passes every task whose only rules are "bins nothing" and "stays still": 3 (sandwich), 10 (stop), 11 (hmm…) and 12 (purple). That's 4/12, only one behind the word counter! It fails 2 (no "(1, 5)") and 8 (no question). A score only means something next to the tasks behind it.

Why are "one action only" failures worse news than "heard the wrong intent" failures?

More training data can fix wrong intents. But a brain that can only output one action can never do "the red and blue blocks", however well it's trained. You'd have to change its output format, like the planner's list of actions.

For "where's the green one?", the attention model wrote put green, and the action passed Lesson 10's safety check. Was the safety check broken?

No. It did its job: it checks that an action is allowed and about a real block. "Put the green block in the bin" is both. Safety checks catch impossible and dangerous actions; only an evaluation like the bake-off catches allowed-but-wrong ones.

Give one reason a real robot would keep a fast local brain even if a world-class model passed every task.

Reflexes like "stop" can't wait for the internet; the robot must still work when the Wi-Fi drops; and every request to a big model costs money and time.
