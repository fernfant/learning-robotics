Start here

A still phone always feels gravity. The way gravity splits across the phone's sides tells you how it's tilted. The bumps tell you about steps.

Accelerometer
A tiny sensor that measures pushes and pulls, including gravity, in m/s². About 50 readings a second.
Axis
One of the phone's three directions: x, y and z. Each reading has one number per axis.
Magnitude
How strong the reading is, whatever direction it points.
Moving average
Smoothing: each new value is the average of the last few.
Peak
A point higher than its neighbours: the top of a bump.

1. The idea

💡 The idea: gravity is an arrow the phone can measure

Hold a glass of water and tilt it. The water stays level, because gravity always pulls straight down. A phone feels that same pull, about 9.8 m/s², even sitting still. It measures it along its own three edges: x, y and z. Stand the phone up and all of it lands on x. Lay it flat and none does. Tip it halfway and it splits.

Why do the arrows point up? A still phone feels the floor (or your hand) pushing it up against gravity, so that's what it measures.

So the angle between the reading and the x axis is the tilt. Our real recording comes from a phone clipped to someone's waist, x pointing up when they stand. Here's what it found:

Standing is nearly upright, sitting leans back, lying is nearly on its side. (You'll print these numbers yourself in step 5.)

How strong is the arrow? Its length, whichever way it points. That length is the magnitude. Walk 3 squares across and 4 up, and the straight line back is 5 long:

Notice gravity isn't exactly 9.81 in the real recording (its six still parts read 8.64 to 10.19 m/s²): real sensors are a little off, like in week 2.

💡 The idea: every step is a bump

When you walk, your body bounces: up, down, up, down. The phone feels each bounce as a bump in the reading's strength. Count the bumps and you've counted steps. Two tricks make it work: smooth the wobbly signal first, and ignore a second bump that comes too soon after the last step.

● Counted step (6 in 3 s) · ○ too soon after a step: ignored

Real data from 171–174 s of the recording. Grey: the raw strength, wobbly. Purple: smoothed over 5 readings. Each step makes two bumps, 6–8 readings apart, so the counter ignores any bump less than 15 readings after the last step it counted.

Smoothing with a moving average: slide a small window along the readings, and write down the average of what's inside. Here the window is 3 wide:

The readings jump 1 to 9. The smoothed ones only wobble 3.7 to 6.3.

  5. count_steps(values, threshold, min_gap) counts peaks. A sample counts as a step if it is:
    • above threshold,
    • bigger than the sample before it,
    • at least as big as the sample after it,

    The first and last samples never count: they don't have two neighbours.

5. Real-world challenge: record yourself

🌍 Real-world challenge: record with phyphox

phyphox is a free app (iPhone and Android) that records your phone's sensors and saves the numbers. Ask an adult before you install it.

  1. Open phyphox and choose Acceleration with g. (Not the one "without g": we want gravity.)
  2. Turn the phone on its side and hold it against your tummy, screen facing out. Press ▶ and check the x graph: standing still, x should be about +9.8. If y is about 9.8 instead, turn the phone onto its side. If x is about −9.8, turn it the other way up.
  3. Press ▶ to start fresh. Stand still for 10 s, sit for 10 s, lie down for 10 s, then stand up and walk for 20 s, keeping the phone in place. Press ⏸.
  4. Tap the ⋮ menu, Export Data, and choose CSV (Comma, decimal point). Send it to your computer (email it to yourself, or ask an adult). If it comes as a .zip, open it.

Check yourself

A phone lies still on a table. Why doesn't it read 0?

Gravity. A still phone always feels about 9.8 m/s². Only a phone in free fall (dropped) reads close to 0.

Predict: tilt_degrees(0, 0, 9.81)?

90. All of gravity is on z, none on x, so x is flat.

Predict: moving_average([0, 0, 9], 3)?

[0.0, 0.0, 3.0]. The last value averages 0, 0 and 9.

Why do we need min_gap?

Each step makes two bumps a few readings apart (look at the chart). Without min_gap, many steps would be counted twice.

The walks give 1.8 steps a second. Why does describe say 0.83?

It divides by the whole 236 s, including all the standing, sitting and lying. Only about 67 s of it is walking.

Want to know more?

Why the same posture gives different tilts

The phone shifts on the belt, and people sit back or lean forward by different amounts. In the second lying part the tilt is 93°, just past flat. Real data is messy. That's why robots smooth, average and check, as you did in week 2.

Why gravity reads 8.64 to 10.19, not 9.81

Cheap sensors have small errors in each axis, and the person was not perfectly still. A robot fixes this by calibrating: measuring when it knows the true answer, and correcting.

Which way did it lean?

Tilt only says how far x is from up, which is why 90° and −90° look the same in the demo. The direction of the lean comes from comparing y and z, with math.atan2(y, z).

Where the data comes from

Experiment 1, user 1 of the UCI "Smartphone-Based Recognition of Human Activities and Postural Transitions" dataset by Reyes-Ortiz, Anguita, Oneto and Parra, under CC BY 4.0 (archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/341). We trimmed it to the first 11,800 readings, converted g to m/s², and added the activity labels. It uses the same CSV layout as phyphox.

What real robots do with this

Every drone, self-balancing scooter and walking robot has an accelerometer, used exactly like this to find "down". Its weakness is that any shake looks like a tilt. In week 6 you add a second sensor, the gyroscope, and blend the two.
