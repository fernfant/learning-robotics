Start here

Walk across a room with your eyes shut, counting steps: smooth, but you slowly go off course. Peek every few seconds: you see where you really are, but a glance while walking is shaky. Do both and you walk straight. That is sensor fusion.

Gyroscope
Measures how fast the phone turns, in degrees per second.
Accelerometer
Feels gravity, so it knows which way is down. Shakes when you move.
Drift
A tiny error that adds up over time.
Complementary filter
Trust the gyro from moment to moment, and the accelerometer over the long run.

1. The idea

💡 The gyro adds up, and drifts

The gyroscope says "turning 2 degrees a second". To get the angle, add up the turning: every 1/50 of a second, add how much it turned in that moment. It's smooth, even while you move. But this phone's gyro reads −0.155 degrees a second when it is perfectly still. That tiny error gets added in every time:

Real recording, standing still, 5–25 s. Blue: the accelerometer, steady at about −7.5°. Orange: the gyro, added up: it ends at −10.0°, so it drifted 2.5°.

2.5° off after 20 s. At that rate, about 93° off after 10 minutes: the robot would think it was lying on its side.

💡 The accelerometer never drifts, but it shakes

Gravity always pulls down. The accelerometer feels it as two parts, along the phone's two edges: an x part and a y part. Tilt the phone and the parts change, so they give you the angle straight away. No adding up, so no drift.

Not the same angle as week 3. Week 3's tilt (9° standing) was how far the phone's x edge is from straight up. This week's angle (about −7.5° standing) uses only x and y: how far the phone is turned like a steering wheel, in the screen's own plane. That is atan2(y, x). Different question, different number, same sensor.

But every step you take is a push the accelerometer can't tell apart from gravity:

Real recording, walking, the first 4 s of 150–162 s. Blue: the accelerometer's angle. Dashed: the true angle (the walk's average).

While walking, it wobbles 9.5° on average around the true angle.

💡 Trust the gyro now, the accelerometer over time

Every reading, mix the two: 98% gyro, 2% accelerometer. Slide alpha to change the mix:

alpha 1 = gyro only (drifts). alpha 0 = accelerometer only (shakes). The answer is in between.

Think of walking with your eyes shut and peeking now and then. Each peek nudges you only a little, so one shaky step barely matters. But 50 nudges a second add up, so the accelerometer slowly pulls back any drift.

💡 The wrap-around trap

Angles go round a dial: past 179° comes −180°, not 181°. In this recording, while lying down, the accelerometer read 177.8° and, 0.02 s later, −179.6°. They're only 2.7° apart. But subtract the numbers and you get 357°. Average them and you get −0.9°: pointing the opposite way!

Blue dots: the two readings, side by side on the left. Their true halfway is ✓ 179.1°, also on the left. ✗ The plain average, −0.9°, is on the right: it went the long way round (357°).

The fix: wrap() every difference into −180…180. Then the gap between 177.8° and −179.6° comes out as 2.7°, the short way. Your complementary uses it.

Check yourself

With alpha 1, what is the fused line? The gyro alone: (1 − 1) = 0, so the accelerometer is never used.

Why not just use the accelerometer and average out the shake? Averaging makes it slow: when the robot really turns, the average lags behind. The gyro sees turns instantly.

What is wrap(−179.6 − 177.8)? 2.6 (2.7 with the unrounded readings): the short way round. Without wrap it's −357.4.

The gyro drifts 2.5° in 20 s. Why doesn't the fused angle? Every reading, 2% of the gap to the accelerometer is closed. Over one second (50 readings) that pulls back about 64% of any drift.

Want to know more?

The best alpha, on this walk: 0 → 9.5°, 0.5 → 7.7°, 0.9 → 3.0°, 0.95 → 2.2°, 0.98 → 2.1°, 0.99 → 2.5°, 1 → 5.9°. Too low and you get the shake. Too high and you get the drift.

A real limit. load stops at 91 s. After that, the phone lies so flat that one angle stops making sense: a 3D object needs three angles. Real phones and drones use quaternions to avoid this, and a Kalman filter, a cleverer mix that works out how much to trust each sensor as it goes.

Where you meet this: self-balancing scooters, drones, and your phone's screen rotation all fuse a gyro with an accelerometer.
