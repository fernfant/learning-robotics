Start here

A real robot can't see where it is. It counts how far each wheel turned, adds up all the little moves, and steers by how far off it is.

Walk across a dark room counting your steps. You have a good guess where you are, but each step is a little off, so the guess gets worse the longer you walk. That's odometry, and the growing error is drift.

Kinematics
How wheel speeds turn into the robot's movement.
Encoder
A sensor that counts how far a wheel has turned, in "ticks".
Odometry
Adding up every little wheel move to guess where you are.
PID
A steering rule: push towards the goal, remember old errors, brake before you overshoot.

1. Two wheels

💡 The idea

Think of a rowing boat. Both oars pull the same: it goes straight. One pulls harder: it turns. Pull opposite ways: it spins on the spot. A two-wheeled robot works exactly the same way.

The orange bars show how fast each wheel rolls. The robot goes forward by the average of the two wheels, and turns by the difference. The faster wheel goes round the outside, so a faster right wheel turns the robot to its left, just like the boat.

2. Counting wheel turns

💡 The idea

Stick a mark on a bike wheel and count how many times it passes your foot. One full turn rolls the bike forward one circumference. An encoder does this automatically, with lots of marks.

The encoder counts what the motor did. It can't tell if the wheel slipped on the floor. Hold that thought.

3. Odometry: adding up little arrows

💡 The idea

Every twentieth of a second the robot turns its wheel ticks into one small arrow ("I moved this far, I turned this much") and adds it to the last one. Each arrow is a tiny bit wrong, so the chain of arrows slowly bends away from the true path.

Here is the real thing, from lesson23_wheels.py: the robot drives a square loop 3 times using only its encoders. The floor doesn't slip at all here. The only problem is that the left wheel is 2% bigger than the robot believes.

The robot (purple, dashed) believes it drove the same neat square three times and ended back at the start. Really (green) each lap turned a little too much, and it ended 1.15 m away. A slippy floor makes it worse: 1.21 m with 5% slip, 1.43 m with 15%.

4. PID: push, remember, brake

💡 The idea

To turn to face a direction, steer by the error: how far you still have to go.

The motors have momentum (they take about a quarter of a second to change speed). So pushing hard with P alone swings straight past the goal, like a swing pushed too hard. Adding D brakes before it gets there. Real runs from lesson23_wheels.py, turning from 0° to 90°:

P alone with kp 20 overshoots by 43°. PD (kp 20, kd 1.5) overshoots by only 6° and settles within 2° of the goal after 0.6 s. Cruise control in cars and drones use the same rule.

🛠 Build
  1. move_pose(pose, d_left, d_right, wheel_base): one little arrow. pose is (x, y, heading) with the heading in radians. Go forward by the average of the wheels (d), turn by the difference. Then split that one arrow into its x part and its y part, like its shadows on the two walls: The lines to type: 
    d = (d_left + d_right) / 2
dtheta = (d_left - d_right) / wheel_base
x += d * cos(heading + dtheta / 2)
y += d * sin(heading + dtheta / 2)
heading += dtheta
    Why d_left - d_right? A faster right wheel turns the robot to its left. In this room y grows down the screen, so headings grow clockwise, and turning left makes the heading smaller.

🛠 Build
  1. errors_to(pose, target): how far to the target, and how much to turn. A target 35° to your left is also 325° to your right. Always pick the short way: The direction to the target is math.atan2(ty - y, tx - x). Subtract the heading to get the turn a, then wrap it into −π…π (the short way) with this line: 
    (a + math.pi) % (2 * math.pi) - math.pi
    Return (distance, heading_error).

🛠 Build
  1. pid_step(state, error, dt, kp, ki, kd): one step of push, remember, brake. state is (integral, previous_error), or None the very first time (then there's no derivative yet, so use the current error as the previous one). In plain words:
    • push (P) = the error now;
    • remember (I, the integral) = all the errors so far, added up;
    • brake (D, the derivative) = how much the error changed since last step, per second.
    The lines to type: 
    integral   = integral + error * dt
derivative = (error - previous_error) / dt
output     = kp * error + ki * integral + kd * derivative
return output, (integral, error)

8. Real-world challenge: be the encoder

🌍 Real-world challenge

With a partner: in a clear room, walk a square of 4 steps per side with your eyes closed, counting steps and turning 90° at each corner. Your partner walks beside you to keep you safe and puts a coin where you stop. How far from the start are you? Do it 3 laps: does the gap grow?

On your own: make a mark on the side of a tin can. Measure its radius. Predict how far 5 full turns will roll it (5 × 2 × π × radius), then roll it on the floor and measure. Try it on a rug too: that's wheel slip.

9. Check yourself

Both encoders count 360 ticks, but the robot turned a little. How?

The encoders count what the motors did. The wheels can slip, or one can be a bit bigger than the robot thinks (like the 2% left wheel), so the floor sees something different.

Why does odometry get worse the longer you drive?

Each little arrow adds its small error to all the earlier ones, and nothing ever corrects them. A small heading error also sends every later arrow off in the wrong direction.

Why wrap the heading error into −π…π?

A target 35° to the left is also 325° to the right. Without wrapping, the robot might spin almost all the way round.

P alone overshoots. Why not just make kp small?

kp 2 barely overshoots (2.7°) but takes 1.9 s to settle. PD gets both: a strong push and a brake, settling in 0.6 s.

Want to know more?
  • The I in PID. Today ki is 0. The integral adds up an error that won't go away (like a steady push from a slope) and slowly pushes harder. Too much I makes things wobble.
  • Why heading + dtheta / 2? On a curve, the robot's average direction during the move is halfway between the old heading and the new one. Using it makes each little arrow more accurate.
  • How real robots fix drift. They mix odometry with other sensors: a gyro (like your week 6 fusion), and a laser scanner that sees the walls. In week 11 your robot makes a map with its scanner, and in week 12 it uses the map to find itself again.
  • Directions in this room. y grows down the screen, as in the grid. That flips the way angles look: a positive heading change turns the robot clockwise on screen, to its right. A faster right wheel turns it left, so its heading gets smaller, exactly as a real robot would.
