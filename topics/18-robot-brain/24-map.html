Start here

The robot's laser says "a wall 1.3 m away, a bit to my right". Turn that into a spot on a map, do it thousands of times, and a map appears.

Imagine you're blindfolded in a room with a very long stick. You poke in every direction and feel where the stick stops. Each poke tells you two things: the air along the stick is empty, and there's something solid at the end. Write every poke down on squared paper and you get a map. That's exactly what your robot does today.

Frame
A point of view for measuring angles and positions. "To my right" (robot frame) is not the same as "to the east" (room frame).
Laser scanner
A sensor that spins and measures the distance to the nearest thing in many directions. Yours has 36 beams, one every 10°.
Occupancy map
A grid where each square says "wall", "free" or "don't know yet".

1. The idea

💡 The idea · two points of view

If a friend says "the ball is on my left", you can only find it if you know which way your friend is facing. The laser measures angles from where the robot faces. The map uses the room's x and y. To go from one to the other, add the robot's heading to the beam's angle:

The robot faces −30°. Its beam is +60° to its right. In the room, the beam points −30° + 60° = 30°.

Then walk along the beam from where the robot stands. A sloping walk is the same as two straight ones: some steps across (x), then some steps down (y).

Because y grows down the screen, a positive angle turns clockwise, which is to the robot's right. In step 1 of the Build, cos and sin work out the two steps for you.

💡 The idea · every beam votes

Each beam is a poke with the stick. The squares it passes through get a free vote. The square where it stops gets a wall vote. Here are four beams in a small practice room (the numbers come from the lesson's own squares_passed code):

Zoom in on one beam. How does the code know which squares a beam passed? It walks along the beam in tiny 5 cm steps (the dots) and notes each new square it lands in. The last square is where the beam stopped, so it is the wall, not free:

One scan can be wrong (the laser has a little noise). But after hundreds of scans, each square has lots of votes, and the majority wins: more wall votes than free votes means wall.

💡 The idea · a map appears

The lesson drives the robot around the room, scanning as it goes. If it knows exactly where it is for every scan (cheating, using the hidden true position), every square the map decided is right: 100% of 79:

Here the robot also steers by its true position, so it follows the route cleanly and never bumps into anything. Look at the red ring, the cat's square (4, 1). The official map says floor, but the robot's map says wall: the laser hit something solid there. That's the cat. The map knows something the official map doesn't. (The cat's square doesn't count towards the score.)

💡 The idea · why the map smears

A real robot doesn't know where it is. It uses its odometry from week 9, which drifts (remember the 2% bigger left wheel?). It also steers by that drifting guess, so it wanders off the route and scrapes along walls. Its wheels keep turning and its encoders keep counting, so it never notices. Every scan gets stuck onto the map in the wrong place, so walls get drawn where there's floor, and floor where there's wall. Same room, same laser, only the position is different:

The top wall has vanished, a wall runs down column 3 where there's floor, and the bottom wall is drawn a whole row too high. Only 55% of the squares it decided are right. A good map needs a good idea of where you are. Next week you'll fix the other half: knowing where you are needs a map. Robotics people call doing both at once SLAM.

5. Real-world challenge

🌍 Perfect wheels, still smeared?

Was the 55% all the big wheel's fault? Map with odometry but equal wheels. From project/homework:

python3 -c "from lesson24_map import explore, score; m, _ = explore(False, mismatch=0.0); print(score(m))"

Real output: (0.8026315789473685, 76). That's 80%: much better than 55%, but still short of 100%. The wheels slip a little on every move, and those small errors add up. Odometry alone is never enough.

On your own: map your bedroom on squared paper. Stand in one spot, and measure the distance to the nearest thing in 8 directions with a tape measure. Mark the free squares and the walls. Then take 3 steps, guess where you are, and do it again. If your guess is off, the walls don't line up. That's the smear.

6. Check yourself

The robot is at (1.0, 2.0) facing east (0°). Its straight-ahead beam hits a wall 1 m away. Where is that point in the room?

(2.0, 2.0). Heading 0 plus beam 0 is 0 (east), and 1 m east of x = 1.0 is x = 2.0.

Why does the end square only get a wall vote if the distance is less than 4 m?

4.0 m is the scanner's maximum. A reading of 4.0 means "I hit nothing", so there's no wall to vote for. The squares along the way are still free.

Why is the cat's square (4, 1) a "#" in the map when the official map says floor?

Beams ended in that square, so it got wall votes. Something solid is there: the cat. The robot only knows what its beams reached, and here they reached the cat.

Drive next to the cat in the Play demo. Why does your map find it when the official map doesn't?

The official map was written by hand before the cat sat down. The new map is built from what the laser actually measured today.

Knowing where it is gives 100%. Using odometry gives 55%. What's different?

Where the robot thinks it is. Drifting odometry puts each scan in the wrong place on the map. It also steers by that wrong guess, so it drives a different path and scrapes walls.

7. Want to know more?

Real occupancy maps

Real robots don't just count votes. They keep a probability for each square, often stored as "log-odds" so that adding a vote is just adding a number. Squares are also much smaller than 0.5 m, often a few centimetres.

Faster beam walking

Walking along a beam in 5 cm steps can miss a corner it just clips. Real code uses Bresenham's line algorithm, which visits exactly the squares a line crosses, with no floating point at all.

SLAM

Simultaneous Localisation And Mapping: build the map and work out where you are on it, both at once, each one helping the other. Robot vacuums do this. Next week's particle filter is one of the building blocks.
