Don't know where you are? Make hundreds of guesses, keep the ones that fit what you see, and move them all as you walk. Soon they all point to the same spot.

Imagine waking up in a friend's house in the dark. You can feel a wall close on your left and a doorway ahead. "I could be in the hall, or by the kitchen door." You take a step and feel again. Places that don't fit what you feel get crossed off. After a few steps, only one place fits.

Kidnapped
Robotics word for "picked up and put down somewhere else". The robot's odometry is now useless.
Particle
One guess: an x, a y and a heading.
Resample
Make a new set of guesses by copying the good ones and dropping the bad ones.

1. The idea

💡 The idea · kidnapped!

The robot was at (1, 1). Someone carries it to square (6, 3) and puts it down facing west. Its wheels never turned, so its odometry still says (1, 1). It has last week's map and its laser scanner, nothing else.

💡 The idea · hundreds of guesses

Instead of one guess, make lots. Each dot is a particle: "maybe I'm here, facing this way" (the little line). Spread them over all the free floor:

One of these is bound to be close to the truth. The trick is finding out which.

💡 The idea · score each guess

For every guess, ask: "if I were really here, what would my laser read?" The map can answer that (it's the same ray as week 11). Then compare that pretend scan with the real one. The real robot is at (6, 3) facing west. Guess A is 5 cm away; guess B is at (3, 1):

Guess A's shape matches, so it scores 1.56. Guess B scores about 0.0000000009: almost two billion times worse. Each beam gets a score, and the beam scores are multiplied, so a guess has to explain almost every beam to do well.

💡 The idea · copy the good guesses

Now pick a brand new set of guesses, the same number, choosing at random but in proportion to the scores. Good guesses get picked several times. Bad ones usually don't get picked at all:

Then the robot drives a little. Every guess moves the same way, plus a little randomness (wheels slip). Copies of a good guess spread out slightly, and the next scan decides which copies are best. Score, pick, move, repeat.

💡 The idea · two safety nets

The surprise term. What if one beam hits the cat? The map doesn't know about the cat, so even a perfect guess gets that beam wrong. If that beam scored 0, the whole product would be 0, and the perfect guess would be thrown away. So every beam scores at least 0.05, as if to say "maybe that's something the map doesn't know":

Fresh guesses. What if every guess is wrong? Copying wrong guesses only makes more wrong guesses. So each round, 5% of the guesses are swapped for brand new random ones. If the robot really is somewhere unexpected, a fresh guess will land near it and win.

6. Check yourself

Why can't the kidnapped robot just use its odometry?

Odometry only counts how far the wheels turned. When it's carried, the wheels don't turn, so odometry still thinks it's where it was picked up.

Why multiply the beam scores instead of adding them?

Multiplying means one badly wrong beam drags the whole score down. A guess must explain most beams. With adding, a guess could score well by matching just a few.

What would happen if a beam could score exactly 0?

Any guess with one surprising beam (say, the one that hits the cat) would get a total of 0 and be thrown away, even if it's in exactly the right place.

Why are the plain averages of 170° and −170° a trap?

Both face nearly west, but their plain average is 0°, which faces east. Averaging sines and cosines and using atan2 gives 180°, which is right.

The guess got worse at scans 3 and 4, then suddenly good at scan 6. What could cause that?

If two places in the room give similar scans, the guesses split between them, and their average lands in between. Once the robot has moved, the scans can tell the places apart and one group wins.

7. Want to know more?

Monte Carlo Localisation

This is the real name for what you built. "Monte Carlo" means "using random numbers to solve a problem". Warehouse robots, robot vacuums and self-driving cars all use ideas like this.

Smarter resampling

rng.choices picks each particle independently, so by bad luck a good guess can vanish. Real filters often use "low-variance resampling": one random number, then evenly spaced picks, which keeps good guesses more reliably.

How many particles?

More particles cover more places but cost more time. Clever filters change the number on the fly: lots when lost, fewer once they know where they are.
