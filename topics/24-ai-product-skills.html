Start here (no maths needed)

An AI brain doesn't fail the same way twice. So the most useful job on an AI team is being a really good tester: finding how it fails, and building a cheap check for each kind of failure.

Think of it like this

  • When Robo-Helper gets a command wrong, there are only three reasons. You didn't look at enough real commands (you didn't know people say “pls”). You didn't say clearly what you wanted (should it ask “where?” or guess?). Or you said it clearly and it still got it wrong on a new kind of sentence.
  • The first two you fix yourself, by reading and by writing clearer instructions. Only the third one needs a test that runs again and again.
  • Tests come in prices. A few lines of code that check “is this valid JSON?” cost nothing. Asking another AI to mark the answer costs money, and you have to check that AI too.

You already do this

  1. check.py compares your code's answers with known right answers. That's a reference-based test.
  2. Your week 10 safety layer blocks bad AI answers before the robot moves. That's a guardrail.
  3. The bake-off looks at the room afterwards, not at what the brain says it did. That's an application eval.

Try it

Play the failure sorter: six real-looking Robo-Helper mistakes. For each one, pick which of the three reasons it is.

Words to know

Eval
A repeatable way to measure how often an AI does the right thing.
Trace
Everything that happened for one request: the command, every step the brain took, and the result.
Pass/Fail
Marking each answer just right or wrong, instead of 1 to 5. Easier to agree on.
Judge
A second AI that marks the first one's answers. Only trust it after checking it against your own marks.

2. Why this matters for robotics

A robot with an LLM brain is an AI product with a body. Every warning in these posts gets sharper once outputs move things.

  • Non-determinism: the same command can produce a different plan tomorrow. One passing run proves little; measure rates (Robo-Helper's Module 19 pass^k).
  • Blast radius: the testing post's example is a recommender suggesting out-of-stock items, which spills into inventory and support. For a robot the blast radius is physical, which is why week 10's checks sit before the motors.
  • Regression across model versions: swapping the model behind the planner (week 15) is a change. Re-run the bake-off before trusting it.
  • Gulf of generalization: the evals post's example is a name in the email body taken as the sender. Robo-Helper's version: “tell Sam the red block is in the bin” read as “pick up Sam”.
  • Last line of defence: the agents post's worst mistake was auto-posting without review. Robo-Helper's MCP week keeps the never-break rules on the robot's side of the plug, for the same reason.

3. Deep dive: evals (Analyze → Measure → Improve)

An eval is a systematic measurement of pipeline quality whose result anyone can read without arguing. It's used three ways: background monitoring (spot drift without blocking anything), guardrails (in the path: block, retry or fall back) and improvement (label data, pick few-shot examples, find failures worth an architecture change).

The three gulfs

From the paper “Who Validates the Validators?”, using an email assistant that extracts the sender, summarises requests and files the email:

1 · ComprehensionDeveloper ↔ data. You can't read every input or every output, so you don't know what your traffic really looks like or how it fails.
2 · SpecificationDeveloper ↔ pipeline. The prompt doesn't say what you meant: how long a summary, bullets or prose, display name or address.
3 · GeneralizationData ↔ pipeline. Clear instructions, still wrong on some inputs: a famous name in the body taken as the sender. Never fully closes.

Why evals are hard: every app needs its own; requirements appear only after you see outputs; the right metric isn't obvious; nothing replaces reading real outputs on real data.

Basics before the loop

  • LLM weak spots: algorithmic work (trained on 3-digit sums, fails 5-digit), unreliability, prompt sensitivity, confident falsehoods.
  • Seven prompt parts: role and objective, rules (including what not to do), context, examples, reasoning steps, output format, delimiters. Precision closes the specification gulf.
  • Write prompts yourself at first. Writing forces you to state the spec; outsourcing it early hides your failure modes.
  • Reference-based metrics compare with a known answer (exact match, run the SQL and compare tables). Reference-free ones check rules (no medical advice, the SQL runs, the summary adds nothing absent from the source). Prefer reference-based when you can: cheaper to keep.
  • Application evals beat foundation benchmarks (MMLU and friends help pick a model, not judge your app). A dashboard of generic “helpfulness_score”-style numbers is the smell of an eval gone wrong.
  • Binary pass/fail beats 1–5. Adjacent scale points are subjective and people drift to the middle. For progress, count sub-checks (“4 of 5 expected facts present”). Pairwise and ranking are for comparing systems.

Step 1 · Analyze

  1. About 100 traces. Real ones if you have them. Otherwise synthetic, built in two steps: pick the dimensions where it's likely to fail (feature, persona, how clear the request is), sample tuples of values, then write one query per tuple. Asking an LLM straight for “user queries” gives bland, repetitive ones. Throw away awkward ones aggressively.
  2. Open coding. Read each whole trace and write a short note on the first (most upstream) thing that went wrong. No categories yet. Stop at ≥20 bad traces with no new kinds appearing (saturation).
  3. Axial coding. Cluster the notes into a small, non-overlapping set of binary failure modes. An LLM can propose groupings from 30–50 notes; you check them. Their example: “invented a house feature” and “invented a tour the user never asked for” look alike but differ (wrong facts vs invented intent).
  4. Label. One 0/1 column per failure mode, every trace. Now you have prevalences to prioritise with. Expect to revise the definitions.
🧩 Common pitfalls Unrepresentative data; skipping open coding for generic labels (“hallucination”, “verbosity”); 1–5 scales in early labelling; freezing the failure list too early.

Step 2 · Measure

Fix the specification gaps first, then measure generalization. If the prompt never asked for the price limit, adding it is faster than building a check, and a check would measure your vagueness, not the model. Build evaluators only for failures that persist with clear instructions. Try to give each one a reference-based and a reference-free version, plus “does it run” checks where outputs are executable.

  1. Code assertions: JSON parses, SQL runs, regex for forbidden words, “3 bullets”, “asked for pets, says pets allowed”. Fast, free, deterministic.
  2. Reference comparisons: a golden set of inputs with correct outputs. Good as CI checks.
  3. Execution checks: run the generated code or query against a test database.
  4. LLM-as-judge: only for nuance code can't capture (tone for a persona, faithfulness). One judge per failure mode, pass/fail. Needs 100+ labels and upkeep.

A judge prompt has four parts: one narrow criterion; exact Pass and Fail definitions; a few clear examples of each from your labelled traces; JSON output with a short reason and the verdict.

Build it like a classifier. Split your labelled traces three ways. Train (10–20%): examples you may put in the prompt. Dev (40–45%): measure after each prompt edit. Test (40–45%): touch once, at the end. Aim for 30–50 Pass and 30–50 Fail in dev and test, even if real failures are rarer. Few-shot gains usually plateau after 1–8 examples, so spend the data on evaluation. Any trace in the prompt must stay out of dev and test.

Score it with two rates. TPR = share of human-Pass the judge also passes. TNR = share of human-Fail the judge also fails. Iterate until both are high (they suggest >90%). If stuck: a stronger model, split the criterion into smaller checks, or better labels. Labels from people without context on the app are a common mistake.

Correct the judge's score. On new, unlabelled traces the judge's raw pass rate is biased. The correction is θ̂ = (p_obs + TNR − 1) ÷ (TPR + TNR − 1), clipped to 0–1. If TPR + TNR ≈ 1 the judge is a coin flip and nothing can be corrected. For error bars, bootstrap the test set's (human, judge) pairs: resample, recompute TPR and TNR, re-correct, take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. In their simulation the correction removes the bias, and judge errors mostly widen the interval; better TPR narrows it most. Try it below.

🧩 Judge pitfalls No examples in the prompt; several criteria in one verdict; assuming it “just works” without measuring alignment; testing on the same traces used as examples; never re-checking. They suggest re-labelling a handful of traces weekly and recomputing TPR, TNR and the interval.

Step 3 · Improve

  • Prompts first: clarify, add targeted examples (kept out of the golden set), ask for step-by-step reasoning.
  • Decompose: extract preferences → query tool → pick listings → draft email, each its own call.
  • RAG: query rewriting, chunking, how many documents, re-ranking.
  • Tool schemas: clear descriptions and parameter rules (how to handle “West Berkeley” vs a made-up “Berkeley West”).
  • Guardrails: fast deterministic checks before output; weigh false blocks.
  • Fine-tune last, for persistent, well-defined failures with lots of good examples.

The flywheel: analyze → build evals → put the golden set in CI → ship with monitoring on sampled traffic and pinned judge models → watch corrected failure rates with intervals → spot drift or new failures → re-analyze → add the new case to the golden set and evaluators → improve → redeploy.

🤖 In Robo-Helper check.py is reference-based CI. The week 10 checks (JSON, allow-list, fallback) are code-level guardrails. The bake-off judges by the final room, which is an execution check. Module 19 shows why an unchecked judge misleads (it uses kappa; this post uses TPR/TNR and corrects the rate).

5. Deep dive: testing AI features

Teams write AI code 3–5× faster, the post says, but releases haven't sped up because testing hasn't caught up. Its anecdote: one company's AI feature builds took 4 hours vs 15 minutes for normal features.

Four ways AI breaks traditional testing

  1. Non-deterministic outputs: good on Tuesday, nonsense on Wednesday, both pass the unit tests.
  2. Bigger blast radius: effects spread to systems far from the AI feature.
  3. New test types: functional tests of outputs, load tests on inference endpoints, regression across model versions, chaos tests for when the AI fails.
  4. Scale: thousands of scenarios where suites were built for hundreds.

The model underneath also changes: a new model version can shift behaviour as much as your own code change. Tests must expect drift.

The framework (three parts)

  1. Business-driven evals, written with the spec: e.g. billing issues go to a human, no refunds over a limit without approval.
  2. Automated tests: model performance within bounds, integration with existing systems, load.
  3. Observability and orchestration: one place for all results, run tests on demand, trends over time, different suites for different situations. Without it you can't answer “are we ready to ship?”

The PM's stack, in four boxes: test orchestration (e.g. Testkube, Argo), model/eval tooling (Braintrust, Langfuse, PromptLayer, W&B, Arize), load (k6, Locust, Gatling), observability (Datadog, Grafana, OpenTelemetry). You don't configure them, but you should know which you have and where the gaps are. Load tests also catch cost: one team found a $50K/month bill before launch.

Working with DevOps: agree “ready to ship” before specs; ask about eval coverage and repeat failures during the build; after launch, turn patterns where users abandon AI outputs into new evals. The “self-healing loop” it describes (AI diagnoses a failing test, opens a fix PR, re-runs) is a direction, not a measured result.

Chatbots (Claude Artifacts, ChatGPT) suit one-off, single-file prototypes; coding tools (Cursor, Copilot) suit people shipping to production. A common path: start in a prototyping tool, sync to GitHub, continue in a coding tool.

Old way vs new way

Before, prototypes appeared mostly at design exploration (~75% of teams by their estimate) and rarely at ideation or in PRDs (~5%). Now PMs use them at every stage: to think, to pitch roadmap ideas, in front of customers, attached to the PRD, as a starting point for designers and even for engineers.

How to build one

  1. Context: Figma import beats screenshots beats a napkin photo beats nothing.
  2. A short PRD in a .md file before any code.
  3. Describe the whole, then the parts, with exact behaviour (“clicking Save stores the form and goes to the dashboard”).
  4. Iterate one change at a time, keeping a copy of the last working version.
🧩 Debugging A system prompt about how to build (data model first, mock data before a database, small files, one change at a time, ask when unclear); state the behaviour you want, not the symptom; ask for “a plan, no code yet”; learn what tidy code looks like. To scale: secrets out of the code, real auth, proper hosting, a domain. Many internal tools never need to scale.

7. Deep dive: practical agents for PMs

Twenty-one use cases in four groups, each with a tool pick:

  • Monitoring and analysis: weekly feedback analysis, feature-request tallies, competitor watching (with a “worth telling me?” filter), adoption tracking, anomaly detection, beta feedback (NotebookLM as a free start).
  • Scheduled reporting: weekly metrics drafts you edit, retro prep, post-launch reports at 7 and 30 days.
  • Triggered docs and dev: PRD drafts on a ticket status change, auto-fixing simple tickets as reviewable PRs, prototypes from specs, release notes, changelogs, help docs.
  • Communication: email and Slack drafts, meeting briefs, follow-ups, interview synthesis, launch announcements.

8. Deep dive: the PM operating system in Claude Code (and MCP)

A $49 folder that makes Claude Code a PM's workbench. The structure is the idea worth keeping:

  • CLAUDE.md, the master context Claude reads first (he says he revised it 100+ times).
  • context-library/: company, users, competitors, metrics, strategy, writing style, your own background. Fill it by talking to Claude, not by hand.
  • .claude/skills/: 41 slash commands (/prd-draft, /meeting-notes, /user-interview, /competitor-analysis, /create-tickets …).
  • sub-agents/: 7 reviewers (engineer, designer, exec, legal, researcher, skeptic, customer) to critique a PRD in parallel.
  • templates/: launch checklists, roadmaps, OKRs, retros.
  • MCP connections (setup step 5) to analytics (PostHog, Amplitude, Pendo), tickets (Linear), support, research and calendar, so Claude can query live data. This is the “LLM with MCP” part.

Other tips: keep it in a git repo (version, revert, share with a new PM), use plan mode for big tasks, run separate sessions per initiative, pick cheaper models for simple steps, and “Ralph Wiggum” loops that retry a task until a clear completion signal appears, with an iteration cap.

⚠️ Careful The post's setup starts Claude Code with permission checks switched off. Skip that until you trust a workflow, and never with MCP connections that can write to real systems. Its own FAQ says the same, more quietly.

11. Playbook

  1. Before any eval tool: collect ~100 traces, read them, note the first failure in each, cluster into binary failure modes, count them.
  2. Fix what you never specified in the prompt before automating anything.
  3. Climb the cost ladder: assertions → golden set → execution → judge. One judge per failure mode, pass/fail, measured on held-out labels, corrected and reported with an interval.
  4. Treat a model upgrade as a release: golden set in CI, judge models pinned, re-run before switching.
  5. Prototype to decide, not to ship: plan first, one change at a time, nothing secret in the code.
  6. Agents: one job each, failure alerts, a human before anything customer-facing, small models for sorting.
  7. Keep your context in files (CLAUDE.md, a context folder, skills), in git, and connect data with MCP read-only first.
  8. For the 12-year-old: play the sorter; then, in the bake-off week, write each failed task as one line of open coding and group them.

12. Self-check

A judge passes 95% of outputs. Why isn't 95% the pass rate?

Because the judge makes mistakes in both directions. With its TPR and TNR from a labelled test set, the corrected rate is (p_obs + TNR − 1) ÷ (TPR + TNR − 1). A lenient judge (low TNR) pushes the raw number up.

Why fix specification failures before writing evals?

They're cheap to fix by editing the prompt, and an eval on them would measure how vague you were, not how well the model generalises.

Why pass/fail rather than 1–5?

People disagree on 3 vs 4 and drift to the middle. Binary labels are faster and more consistent; track progress by counting sub-checks.

How should you split 200 labelled traces to build a judge?

Roughly 10–20% train (prompt examples), 40–45% dev (tune), 40–45% test (final, untouched), with 30–50 passes and 30–50 fails in dev and test. Nothing in the prompt may appear in dev or test.

What's open coding, and when do you stop?

Reading whole traces and writing a free note on the first thing that went wrong, without categories. Stop after at least 20 bad traces when no new kinds of failure appear (saturation).

Name the four ways AI features break traditional testing.

Non-deterministic outputs, a wider blast radius, new test types (output quality, inference load, cross-model regression, chaos), and far more scenarios.

Which agent mistake would hurt a robot most, and what's the guard?

Acting without review on something that matters. The guard is checks the AI can't bypass, on the robot's side (week 10's safety layer, the MCP week's last line of defence).
